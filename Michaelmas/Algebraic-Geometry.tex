\subsection{Why scheme theory?}

\textbf{Moduli theory} -- It is better to study families of varieties than one at a time. Or better, all varieties of a given type at the same time.

\underline{Examples of moduli:} The set of all lines in \(\P^2\). A line in \(\bb P^2\) is
\[\{a X_0 + bX_1 + cX_2 = 0\}\]
In particular, as lines are paramterised by the triple \((a,b, c)\), we have a correspondence
\[\P^2_\text{dual} = \text{lines in }\P^2 \bij \bb P^2\]
The same logic applies for degree \(d\) hypersurface in \(\P^n\), and we get
\[\text{degree }d\text{ hypersufaces in }\P^n \bij \P^N\]
where \(N = {n + d \choose d} - 1\). However, there is something wrong with this picture. Some polynomials are of the form
\[f = f_1^2f_2\]
But in this case,
\[\van(f) = \van(f_1f_2)\]
and \(f_1f_2\) is (in general) not of degree \(d\).

\underline{A solution} Take \(U_d \sse \P^N\), where
\[[f] \in U_d \iff f\text{ has no repeated factors}\]
But \(U_d\) is not compact.

\underline{Output of scheme theory} for a fixed projective space \(\P^n\), we obtain a space
\[\text{subvarieties of }\P^n \bij \Var(\P^n) \subsetneq \Hilb(\P^n) \bij \text{ subschemes of }\P^n\]
where \(\Hilb(\P^n)\) is the Hilbert scheme of \(\P^n\), which is compact in the Euclidean topology. That is, the limit of varieties need not be a variety, but limits of schemes are always schemes.

In scheme theory,
\[\van(X_0 + X_1 + X_2) \text{ and }\van((X_0 + X_1 + X_2)^2)\]
are not isomorphic as schemes.

\textbf{Weil conjectures}\index{Weil conjectures} Fix \(f \in \Z[X_0, \dots, X_{n+1}]\) homogeneous. We have
\[X = \van(f) \sse \P^{n+1}_\C\]
We will assume that \(X\) is smooth. That is, \(X\) is a (complex) manifold. In particular, \(X\) is a compact topological space, and so we have numbers \(b_0(X), \dots, b_{2n}(X)\) called the Betti numbers, where
\[b_i(X) = \rank(H_i(X, \Z))\]
In particular, we have the Euler characteristic \(\chi(X)\).

On the other hand, fix a prime number \(p\), and let
\[N_m = \text{number of solutions to }f\text{ in }\bb F_{p^m} = \abs{X(\bb F_{p^m})}\]
Define the Weil \(\zeta\) function
\[\zeta(X; t) = \exp(\sum_m\frac{N_m}{m}t^m)\]

\begin{theorem}
    [Weil conjectures, Grothendieck]
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(\zeta(X; t)\) is a rational function in \(t\)
        \item Moreover,
        \[\zeta(X; t) = \frac{P_0(t)P_2(t)\cdots P_{2n}(t)}{P_1(t)P_3(t)\cdots P_{2n-1}(t)}\]
        where \(\deg(P_i(t)) = b_i(X)\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\underline{Upshot:} The proof is (fundamentally) via scheme theory. We need a space \(\mf X\) which interpolates geometry over \(\C\) and geometry over finite fields.

\section{Beyond varieties}

\subsection{Summary of classical algebraic geometry}

Let \(k = \overline k\) be an algebraically closed field. Define the \emph{affine space}
\[\aff{k}{n} = k^n\]
as a set.

An \emph{affine variety}\index{affine variety} is a subset
\[V = \van(S) \sse \aff k n\]
for some \(S \sse k\poly n\). Note that \(\van(S) = \van(\inner{S})\), where \(\inner{S}\) is the ideal generated by \(S\). By the Hilbert basis theorem, or equivalently, \(k \poly n\) is Noetherian, \(\inner{S}\) is generated by finitely many elements. Moreover,
\[\van(I) = \van\left(\sqrt{I}\right)\]
where \(I\) is the \emph{radical}\index{radical} of \(I\), given by
\[\sqrt{I} = \left\{f \mid f^m \in I \text{ for some }m \in \N\right\}\]

Given varieties \(V \sse \aff k n\), \(W \sse \aff k m\), a \emph{morphism}\index{morphism!of affine varieties} \(\varphi : V \to W\) is a function \(V \to W\), such that if we write \(\varphi = (f_1, \dots, f_m)\), each \(f_i\) is a restriction of a polynomial in \(k \poly n\). An \emph{isomorphism}\index{isomorphism!of affine varieties} \(\varphi : V \to W\) is a morphism, such that \(\psi : W \to V\) also a morphism, \(\psi \circ \varphi = \id_V\), \(\varphi \circ \psi = \id_W\).

The basic correspondence is
\[\frac{\text{affine varieties over }k}{\text{isomorphism}} \bij \text{finitely generated }k\text{-algebras }A\text{ without nilpotent elements}\]

\underline{How?} Given a variety \(V\) (representing an isomorphism class of such), then we can write \(V = \van(I)\). Moreover, we can assume that \(I\) is a radical ideal. We map
\[V \mapsto \frac{k\poly n}{I}\]
Conversely, if \(A\) is a finitely generated nilpotent-free \(k\)-algebra, then by definition,
\[A \cong \frac{k[y_1, \dots, y_n]}{J}\]
where \(J\) is a radical ideal.

Note that we need to check that both maps are well-defined.

\begin{notation}
    The algebra associated to \(V\) is (classically) denoted \(k[V]\), and called the \emph{coordinate ring of \(V\)}\index{coordinate ring}.
\end{notation}

\subsubsection*{Compatibility of morphisms}

Note that we have a correspondence

\[\{\text{morphisms }V \to W\} \bij \{k\text{-algebra homomorphisms }k[W] \to k[V]\}\]

\subsubsection*{Zariski topology}

Suppose \(V = \van(I) \sse \aff k n\) is an affine variety, with coordinate ring \(k[V]\). The \emph{Zariski topology}\index{Zariski topology!of affine varieties} has closed sets
\[V \cap \van(S)\]
where \(S \sse k\poly n\).

If \(V \cong W\) as varieties, then the topological spaces with the Zariski topology are also homeomorphic.

\subsubsection*{Nullstellensatz}

Fix a variety \(V\), and let \(k[V]\) be its coordinate ring. Given a point \(p \in V\), we can produce a homomorphism
\begin{align*}
    \ev_p : k[V] &\to k \\
    \ev_p(f) &= f(p)
\end{align*}
Moreover, \(\ev_p\) is surjective, for example, by taking the constant functions. With this,
\[\mfm_p = \ker(\ev_p) \idealof k[V]\]
is a maximal ideal. So we get a map
\[\left\{\text{points }p \in V\right\} \to \left\{\text{maximal ideals in }k[V]\right\}\]
\underline{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz} \(\implies\) the above map is a bijection.

Therefore, points of \(V\) and maximal ideals in \(k[V]\) are ``the same thing''.

\subsection{Limitations}

\subsubsection*{What is an abstract variety?}

That is, what is a topological space \(X\), such that we have an open cover \(\{U_i\}\), where each \(U_i\) is an affine variety, which is compatible on overlaps? For example, we have projective space \(\P^n\).

\begin{example}
    [non-algebraically closed fields] Take the ideal
    \[I = \inner{x^2 + y^2 + 1} \idealof \R[x, y]\]
    Observe \(\van(I) = \emptyset \sse \R^2\), but \(I\) is prime, so radical. Hence the Nullstellensatz fails in this case.
\end{example}

\subsubsection*{On what topological space is \(R[x, y]/\inner{x^2 + y^2 + 1}\) ``naturally'' the set of functions?}

Or more generally, \(\Z, \Z[x]\) and so on.

\begin{example}
    [why restrict to radical ideals, or nilpotent free algebras]
    Let \(C = \van(y - x^2) \sse \aff k 2\), and \(D = \van(y) \sse \aff k 2\). Then
    \[C \cap D = \van(x, y) = \left\{(0, 0)\right\}\]
    If we set \(D_\delta = \van(y - \delta)\), then \(C \cap D_\delta\) is two points, for all \(\delta \ne 0\).
\end{example}

\subsection{Spectrum of a ring}

Let \(A\) be a (commutative, unital) ring.

\begin{definition}
    [Zariski spectrum] The \emph{Zariski spectrum of \(A\)}\index{Zariski spectrum, \(\Spec A\)} is
    \[\Spec A = \left\{\mfp \idealof A \mid \mfp \text{ is a prime ideal}\right\}\]
\end{definition}

Given a ring homomorphism \(\varphi : A \to B\), we have a induced map (of sets)
\begin{align*}
    \varphi^{-1} : \Spec(B) &\to \Spec A \\
    \mfq &\mapsto \varphi^{-1}(\mfq)
\end{align*}

\underline{Warning:} This would fail if we considered maximal ideals instead. That is, the preimage of a maximal ideal need not be maximal.

Given \(f \in A\) and \(\mfp \in \Spec A\), we have an induced
\begin{align*}
    \overline f \in A/\mfp
\end{align*}
by taking the quotient. Informally, we can evaluate any \(f \in A\) at points \(\mfp \in \Spec(A)\), with the caviat that the codomain of the evaluation map depends on \(\mfp\).

\begin{example}

\subsection{Topology}

Fix \(f \in A\), then define
\[\van(f) = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid f \equiv 0 \pmod \mfp\right\} \sse \Spec(A)\]
or equivalently, \(f + \mfp = 0 \in A/\mfp\), or \(f \in \mfp\). Similarly, for \(J \idealof A\) an ideal,
\[\van(J) = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid J \sse \mfp\right\}\]

\begin{proposition}
    The sets \(\van(J) \sse \Spec A\) ranging over all ideals \(J \idealof A\), form the closed sets of a topology on \(\Spec A\). This topology is called the \emph{Zariski topology}\index{Zariski topology!on \(\Spec A\)}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Easy facts that \(\emptyset = \van(1)\) and \(\Spec A = \van(0)\) are closed. Since
    \[\van\left(\sum_\alpha I_\alpha\right) = \bigcap_\alpha \van(I_\alpha)\]
    the intersections of closed sets is closed. Finally,
    \[\van(I_1 \cap I_2) = \van(I_1) \cup \van(I_2)\]
    \(\supseteq\) is clear, conversely, \(I_1I_2 \sse I_1 \cap I_2 \sse \mfp\), then by primality of \(\mfp\), \(I_1 \sse \mfp\) or \(I_2 \sse \mfp\).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
    Consider \(\Spec\C[x, y]\). A few observations:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(0 \in \Spec\C[x, y]\) is dense in the Zariski topology. That is,
        \[\overline{\{\inner{0}\}} = \Spec\C[x, y]\]
        since every prime ideal contains \(0\).
        \item Consider the prime ideal \(\inner{y^2 - x^3}\). Consider a maximal ideal
        \[\mfm_{a, b} = \inner{x-a, y-b}\]
        When is \(\mfm_{a, b} \in \overline{\{\inner{y^2 - x^3}\}}\)? This holds if and only if \(b^2 = a^3\). See examples sheet 1.
    \end{itemize}
    \begin{remark}
        Points are not closed.
    \end{remark}
\end{example}

\subsection{Functions on opens}

Let \(f \in A\). Define the \emph{distinguished open corresponding to \(f\)}\index{distinguished open, \(U_f\)} to be
\[U_f = \Spec(A) \setminus \van(f)\]

\begin{example}
    If \(A = \C[x]\), then
    \[\Spec A = \C \cup \{\inner{0}\}\]
    Take \(f = x\). Recall that we have a bijection
    \begin{align*}
        \Spec(A) &\bij \C \cup \{\inner{0}\} \\
        (x-a) &\mapsfrom a \in \C \\
        0 &\mapsfrom 0
    \end{align*}
    Then \(\van(x) = \{\mfp \in \Spec A \mid x \in \mfp\} = \{\inner{x}\}\). So
    \[U_x = \Spec(A) \setminus \{\inner{0}\}\]
    As a picture, \(\Spec(\C[x])\) is a line (corresponding to \(\C\)) with a ``generic point'' \(\{0\}\).

    More generally, suppose we fix \(a_1, \dots, a_r \in \C\), with
    \[U = \Spec(A) \setminus \{\inner{x-a_i}\}_{i=1}^r\]
    then
    \(U = U_f\)
    where \(f = \prod_{i=1}^r(x - a_i)\).
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}
    The distinguished opens \(U_f\) for all \(f \in A\) form a basis for the Zariski topology on \(\Spec A\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Examples sheet 1.
\end{proof}

A bit of commutative algebra: Given \(f \in A\), the \emph{localisation of \(A\) at \(f\)}\index{localisation!at \(f \in A\)} is
\[A_f = \frac{A[x]}{\inner{xf - 1}} = A\left[\frac{1}{f}\right]\]

\begin{lemma}
    The distinguished open \(U_f \sse \Spec(A)\) is naturally homeomorphic to \(\Spec(A_f)\), via the ring homomorphism
    \begin{align*}
        j : A &\to A_f
    \end{align*}
    which induces
    \[j^{-1} : \Spec(A_f) \to \Spec A\]
\end{lemma}

\lec

\begin{proof}
    Primes in \(A_f\) are in bijection with primes of \(A\), which miss \(f\), via \(j^{-1}\). To see this, the preimage of a prime ideal is prime, and so if \(\mfq \idealof A_f\) is prime, then \(j^{-1}(\mfa) \idealof A\) is prime. For the converse, given \(\mfp \idealof A\), define
    \[\mfp_f = j(\mfp)A_f = \inner{j(\mfp)}\]
    
    \begin{claim}
        \(\mfp_f\) is a prime exactly when \(f \notin \mfp\). 
    \end{claim}
    \begin{proof}
        [Proof of claim]
        To see this, \(f\) is a unit in \(A_f\), and so if \(f \in \mfp\), then \(\mfp_f\) contains a unit, and so \(\mfp_f = 1\).
    
        But if \(f \notin \mfp\), then
        \[\frac{A_f}{\mfp_f} \cong \left(\frac{A}{\mfp}\right)_{\overline f}\]
        where \(\overline f = f + \mfp\). So
        \[(A/\mfp)_{\overline f} \sse \Frac(A/\mfp)\]
        and so it is an integral domain.
    \end{proof}

    Finally, we can check that this defines a bijection, but this is just basic properties of ideals.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Facts about distinguised opens}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \(U_f \cap U_g = U_{fg}\).
    \item \(U_{f^n} = U_f\) for all \(n \ge 1\).
    \item The rings \(A_f\) and \(A_{f^n}\) (for \(n \ge 1\)) are isomorphic. To see this,
    \[A_f = \frac{A[x]}{\inner{xf - 1}} \qqtext{and} A_{f^n} = \frac{A[y]}{\inner{yf^n - 1}}\]
    The isomorphism is given by
    \[y \mapsto x^n \qqtext{and} x \mapsto f^{n-1}y\]
    which define inverse maps.
    \item \underline{Containment:} \(U_f \sse U_g\) if and only if \(f^n\) is a multiple of \(g\) for some \(n \ge 1\). (Recall if \(f = gf'\), then certainly \(U_f \sse U_g\))
    \begin{proof}
        The ``if'' direction is clear. Conversely, if \(U_f \sse U_g\). That is,
        \[\van(f) \supseteq \van(g)\]
        The set \(\van(f)\), by definition, is the set of all primes, containing \(f\).

        \begin{claim}
            \(\sqrt{\inner{f}} \sse \sqrt{\inner{g}}\).
        \end{claim}

        To see this, the radical of an ideal \(I\) is the intersection of all primes containing \(I\), which we will see in Commutative Algebra.
    \end{proof}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Foreshadowing}

Fix \(A\), we've made an assignment
\begin{align*}
    \text{distinguised opens in }\Spec A &\to \text{rings} \\
    U_f &\mapsto A_f
\end{align*}
The association is functorial, that is, if \(U_{f_1} \sse U_{f_2}\), then we can assume \(f_1^n = f_2f_3\), so \(U_{f_1} = U_{f_1^n} = U_{f_2f_3} \sse U_{f_2}\), and so there is a homomorphism
\[A_{f_2} \to A_{f_1}\]
which is the restriction map.

Can we extend this association to all open sets? See notes.

\section{Sheaves}

\subsection{Presheaves}

Let \(X\) be a topological space.

\begin{definition}
    [presheaf] A \emph{presheaf!of abelian groups}\index{presheaf of abelian groups} is an association
    \begin{align*}
        \left\{\text{Open set in }X\right\} &\bij \text{Abelian Groups} \\
        U \mapsto \mcF(U)
    \end{align*}
    and for \(U \sse V\) opens, we have a homomorphism
    \[\res_U^V : \mcF(V) \to \mcF(U)\]
    called the \emph{restriction map}\index{restriction map}, such that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(\res_U^U = \id\),
        \item \(\res^V_U\res^W_V = \res^W_U\) for \(U \sse V \sse W\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    For any space \(X\), take
    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to \R \mid f\text{ continuous}\right\}\]
    with \(\res^V_U(f) = f\vert_U\).
\end{example}

We can make an analogous definition for sheaves of rings, sets, \(R\)-modules, and so on.

\begin{definition}
    [morphism of presheaves] A \emph{morphism \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) of presheaves}\index{morphism!of presheaves} on \(X\) is, for each \(U \sse X\) open, a homomorphism
    \[\varphi(U) : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\]
    compatible with restrictions, that is, the diagram

\[\begin{tikzcd}
	{\mcF(V)} && {\mcG(V)} \\

	\arrow["{\res^V_U}", from=1-3, to=3-3]

commutes, for all \(U \sse V\).
\end{definition}

In terms of category theory, the opens in \(X\) form a category, with morphisms given by inclusions. In this case, a presheaf is a contravariant functor \(\text{Opens}(X) \to \text{Ab}\), and a morphism is a natural transformation.

A morphism \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) is \emph{injective}\index{injective!morphism of presheaves} (resp. \emph{surjective}\index{surjective!morphism of presheaves}) if \(\varphi(U) : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\) is injective (resp. surjective) for all \(U \sse X\).

\subsection{Sheaves}

\begin{definition}
    [sheaf]
    A \emph{sheaf}\index{sheaf} is a presheaf \(\mcF\) on \(X\), such that
    \begin{enumerate}[label=S\arabic*.]
        \item Supppose \(U \sse X\) open, \(\{U_i\}\) an open cover of \(U\), then for \(s \in \mcF(U)\), with
        \[s \vert_{U_i} = \res^U_{U_i}s = 0\]
        for all \(i\), then \(s = 0\).
        \item Suppose \(U \sse X\) is open, \(\{U_i\}\) an open cover of \(U\), given \(s_i \in \mcF(U_i)\), with
        \[s_i\vert_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j\vert_{U_i \cap U_j}\]
        then there exists \(s \in \mcF(U)\) with \(s\vert_{U_i} = s_i\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    These axioms imply that \(\mcF(\emptyset) = 0\).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [morphism of sheaves]
    A \emph{morphisms of sheaves}\index{morphism!of sheaves} \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) is just a morphism of the underlying presheaves.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    If \(X\) is a topological space, and
    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to \R \text{ continuous}\right\}\]
    Then \(\mcF\) is a sheaf.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [non-example] Let \(X = \C\) with the usual Euclidean topology. Let
    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to \C \text{ holomorphic and bounded}\right\}\]
    The issue here is that function being bounded is not a local property. That is, we can glue bounded functions together and get an unbounded function.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [non-example] Fix an abelian group \(G\), and set
    \[\mcF(U) = G\]
    This is called the \emph{constant presheaf}\index{constant presheaf}. If \(U_1, U_2\) are disjoint, then
    \[\mcF(U_1 \cup U_2) = \mcF(U_1) \oplus \mcF(U_2) = G \oplus G \ne G\]
    (unless \(G = 0\) that is).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [constant sheaf]
    Let \(G\) be an abelian group, with the discrete topology. Then define
    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to G \text{ continuous}\right\} = \left\{f : U \to G \text{ locally constant}\right\}\]
    This is called the \emph{constant sheaf}\index{constant sheaf}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    If \(V\) is an irreducible variety, define
    \[\mcO_V(U) = \left\{f \in k(V) \mid f \text{ is regular at all }p \in U\right\}\]
    Here, regular at \(p\) means \(f = g/h\), \(g, h \in k[V]\), with \(h(p) \ne 0\). \(\mcO_X\) is called the \emph{structure sheaf}\index{structure sheaf!of a variety} of \(V\). This is a sheaf as being regular is a local condition.
\end{example}

\subsection{Basic constructions}

\begin{definition}
    [section]
    Let \(\mcF\) be a sheaf, a \emph{section}\index{section} \(s\) of \(\mcF\) on \(U\) is an element \(s \in \mcF(U)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [Stalks] Fix \(p \in X\), and \(\mcF\) a presheaf on \(X\). The \emph{stalk of \(\mcF\) at \(p\)}\index{stalk} is
    \[\mcF_p = \frac{\left\{(U, s) \mid U \text{ open neighbourhood of }p, s \in \mcF(U)\right\}}{\sim}\]
    where \((U, s) \sim (V, t)\) if there exists \(W \sse U \cap V\) an open neighbourhood of \(p\), such that
    \[s \vert_W = t\vert_W\]
    Elements of \(\mcF_p\) are called \emph{germs}\index{germ}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Consider the affine line \(\bb A^1\), then
    \[\mcO_{\bb A^1, 0} = \left\{\frac{f(t)}{g(t)} \ \bigg\vert\ g(0) \ne 0\right\} \sse k(t)\]
    Equivalently, we can consider this as the localisation \(k[t]_{(t)}\).
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    Suppose \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\) is a morphism of sheaves on \(X\), such that for all \(p \in X\), the induced map
    \[f_p : \mcF_p \to \mcG_p\]
    is an isomorphism. Then \(f\) is an isomorphism.
\end{proposition}

Note that
\[f_p((U, s)) = (U, f_U(s))\]
which is well defined by definition of a morphism of sheaves.

\begin{proof}
    We will show that
    \[f_U : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\]
    is an isomorphism for all \(U\). Once we know this, we will define \(f^{-1}\) by
    \[(f^{-1})_U = f_U^{-1}\]

    \textbf{\(f_U\) is injective.} Suppose \(s \in \mcF(U)\) has \(f_U(s) = 0\). By injectivity of \(f_p\), \((U, s) = 0\) in \(\mcF_p\) for all \(p \in U\). This means that for all \(p \in U\), there exists an open neighbourhood \(U_p\) of \(p\) such that \(s\vert_{U_p} = 0\). This defines a cover of \(U\). Hence by S1, \(s = 0\). 

    \textbf{\(f_U\) is surjective.} Given \(t \in \mcG(U)\), for each \(p \in U\), we have \((U_p, s_p) \in \mcF_p\), with \(f_p(U_p, s_p) = (U, t) \in \mcG_p\). By shrinking \(U_p\) if necessary, we can assume \(f_{U_p}(s_p) = t\vert_{U_p}\). For points \(p, q \in U\),
    \[f_{U_p \cap U_q}(s_p\vert_{U_p \cap U_q} - s_q\vert_{U_p \cap U_q}) = t\vert_{U_p \cap U_q} - t\vert_{U_p \cap U_q} = 0\]
    Thus bu injectivity of \(f_{U_p \cap U_q}\),
    \[s_p\vert_{U_p \cap U_q} = s_q\vert_{U_p \cap U_q}\]
    Thus by S2, there exists \(s \in \mcF(U)\), with \(s\vert_{U_p} = s_p\). Now
    \[f_U(s) \vert_{U_p} = f_U(s\vert_{U_p}) = f\vert_{U_p}(s_p) = t\vert_{U_p}\]
    Thus, \(f_U(s) = t\) by S1.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    There is an asymmetry in the proof here, we need to show injectivity before surjectivity.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Kernels, Cokernels, etc.}

Let \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) be a morphism of presheaves. Then we can define presheaves \(\ker(\varphi), \im(\varphi), \coker(\varphi)\)\index{kernel!as a presheaf}\index{image!as a presheaf}\index{cokernel!as a presheaf}, where on an open set \(U\), we define
\begin{align*}
    \ker(\varphi)(U) &= \ker(\varphi_U : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)) \\
    \coker(\varphi)(U) &= \coker(\varphi_U) \\
    \im(\varphi)(U) &= \im(\varphi_U)
\end{align*}
These are all presheaves.

\underline{Exercise:} The presheaf kernel for a morphism \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) between sheaves is a sheaf\index{kernel!as a sheaf}.

However, in general, \(\coker(\varphi)\) is not a sheaf.

\begin{example}
    Let \(X = \C\) with the usual Euclidean topology, and let
    \[\mcO_X(U) = \left\{f : U \to \C \text{ holomorphic}\right\}\]
    with addition, and set
    \[\mcO_X^*(U) = \left\{f : U \to \C \text{ holomorphic nowhere vanishing}\right\}\]
    with multiplication. We have a morphism of sheaves \(\exp : \mcO_X \to \mcO_X^*\), given by
    \[\exp(f)(z) = \exp(f(z))\]
    The kernel of \(\exp\) is \(2\pi i \Z\), where \(\Z\) is the constant sheaf. But the cokernel is not a sheaf. Let \(U_1 = \C \setminus \Ico{0, \infty}\) and \(U_2 = \C \setminus \Ioc{-\infty, 0}\). Let \(U = U_1 \cup U_2 = \C \setminus 0\).

    Let \(f(z) = z\). Then \(f \in \mcO_x^*(U)\), and is not in the image of \(\exp : \mcO_X(U) \to \mcO_X^*(U)\), since \(\log(z)\) is not defined on \(U\). Thus \(f\) defines a non-zero section of \(\coker(\exp)(U)\). But if we restrict to \(U_i\), then \(f\) is in the image of \(\exp\). With this, \(f\vert_{U_i} = 1 \in \coker(\exp)(U_i)\). Hence \(\coker(\exp)\) does not satisfy S1.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    [sheaf image, sheaf cokernel]
    Let \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) be a morphism of sheaves. We define the \emph{sheaf cokernel}\index{cokernel!as a sheaf} and the \emph{sheaf image}\index{image!as a sheaf} of \(\varphi\) to be \(\coker(\varphi)^{\sh}\) and \(\im(\varphi)^{\sh}\) respectively.
\end{definition}

We say that a morphism \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\) is \emph{injective}\index{injective!morphism of sheaves} if \(\ker(f) = 0\), and \emph{surjective}\index{surjective!morphism of sheaves} if \(\im(f) = \mcG\).

\underline{Warning:} \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\) being surjective does \emph{not} imply that the map \(f_U : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\) is surjective for all \(U\)\footnote{i.e. a surjective morphism of sheaves need not be surjective as a morphism of presheaves...}.

\section{Schemes}

tl;dr: \(\Spec(A)\) with a sheaf \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}\), with \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}(U_f) = A_f\). Globalise to get a scheme.

\begin{definition}
    [localisation]
    Let \(A\) be a ring, \(S \sse A\) closed under multiplication. The \emph{localisation of \(A\) at \(S\)}\index{localisation!at a subset} is
    \[S^{-1}A = \frac{\left\{(a, s) \mid a \in A, s \in S\right\}}{\sim}\]
    where \((a, s) \sim (a', s')\) if and only if there exists \(s'' \in S\) such that
    \[s''(as' - a's) = 0\]
\end{definition}

For example, consider \(S = \{1, f, f^2, \dots\}\), or \(S = A \setminus \mfp\) where \(\mfp\) is a prime ideal.

\begin{remark}
    Note that the natural map \(A \to S^{-1}A\) need not be injective.
\end{remark}

\underline{What is going to happen?} We will define a sheaf \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}\) on \(\Spec(A)\), such that
\begin{itemize}
    \item The stalk at a prime \(\mfp\) is the localisation \((A \setminus \mfp)^{-1}A\),
    \item if \(U_f\) is a distinguised open, then \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}(U_f) = A_f\).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sheaf on a base}

Fix a topological space \(X\), and \(\mcB\) a basis for the topology.

\begin{definition}
    [sheaf on a base]
    A \emph{sheaf \(F\) on the base \(\mcB\)}\index{sheaf!on a base} consists of assignments \(B_i \mapsto F(B_i)\), with restriction maps \(F(B_I) \to F(B_j)\) whenever \(B_J \sse B_i\), satisfying the usual relations when \(B_i \sse B_j \sse B_k\) and \(B_i = B_j\). Moreover, we have the additional axioms:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=SB\arabic*.]
        \item If \(B = \bigcup_i B_i\), with \(B, B_i \in \mcB\), \(f, g \in F(B)\), with \(f\vert_{B_i} = g\vert_{B_i}\) for all \(i\), then \(f = g\).
        \item If \(B = \bigcup_i B_i\), with \(B, B_i \in \mcB\), with \(f_i \in F(B_i)\), such that for all \(B' \sse B_i \cap B_j\), \(f_i \vert_{B'} = f_j \vert_{B'}\), then there exists \(f \in F(B)\) with \(f\vert_{B_i} = f_i\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \index{sheaf!from a sheaf on a base}
    Let \(F\) be a sheaf on a base \(\mcB\) of \(X\). Then this uniquely determines a sheaf \(\mcF\), by \(\mcF(B_i) = F(B_i)\), agreeing with restriction maps.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    We will define the stalks of \(\mcF\) first. Set
    \[\mcF_p = \frac{\{(s_B, B) \mid p \in B \in \mcB, s_B \in F(B)\}}{\sim}\]
    where \((s_B, B) \sim (s_{B'}, B')\) if there exists \(B'' \sse B \cap B'\) such that \(s_B\vert_{B''} = s_{B'}\vert_{B''}\).

    We can then use the trick from sheafification, that is,
    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{(f_p \in \mcF_p)_{p \in U} \mid (*)\right\}\]
    where the condition \((*)\) is such that: for every \(p \in U\), there exists a basic open \(B\) containing \(p\), and \(s \in F(B)\), with \(s_q = f_q\) for all \(q \in B\). This is clearly a sheaf.

    By the sheaf axioms, the natural maps \(F(B) \to \mcF(B)\) is an isomorphism by the sheaf axioms.
\end{proof}

\lec

Recall \(\Spec(A)\) is a topological space, with distinguised open sets \(\{U_f\}_{f \in A}\). Moreover, \(U_f = U_g\) if and only if \(f^n = ga\) and \(g^m = fb\) for some \(m, n \in \N, a, b \in A\). Thus, if \(U_f = U_g\), then \(A_f \cong A_g\). Therefore, the assignment
\[U_f \mapsto A_f\]
is well-defined.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:sheaf-of-rings-on-base}
    The assignment \(U_f \to A_f\) defines a sheaf of rings on the base \(\{U_f\}\) of \(\Spec(A)\).
\end{proposition}

An immediate consequence is that \(\Spec(A)\) inherits a sheaf of rings, denoted \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}\), called the \emph{structure sheaf}.

\begin{proof}
    [Prelude] Suppose \(\{U_{f_i}\}_{i \in I}\) covers \(\Spec(A)\), then there exists a finite subcover. That is, \(\Spec(A)\) is quasicompact. This is on examples sheet 1, but also:

    Since the \(U_{f_i}\) cover, there is no prime ideal \(\mfp \idealof A\) containing all \(f_i\). Equivalently,
    \[\sum_{i \in I}\inner{f_i} = \inner{1}\]
    But we can write \(1\) as a finite sum \(1 = \sum a_i f_i\). But then if \(J \sse I\) are the indices with \(a_i \ne 0\), then \(\{U_{f_i}\}_{i \in J}\) cover. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{prop:sheaf-of-rings-on-base}] We need to check SB1 and SB2. We will check these for the basis open \(B = \Spec(A)\). The general case is similar (replace \(A\) with \(A_f\)).

    \textbf{SB1:} Suppose we have a cover
    \[\Spec(A) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_{f_i}\]
    By the prelude, it suffices to consider the finite case. Given \(s \in A\) such that \(s \vert_{U_{f_i}} = 0\) for all \(i\), then by the definition of localisation, we have that \(f_i^m s =0\) for some \(m\) large enough. But \(\inner{1} = \inner{f_i^m}_{i=1}^n\) for any \(m > 0\), as the \(U_{f_i}\) cover, which then implies the \(U_{f_i^m}\) cover.

    With this,
    \[s = s \cdot 1 = s \cdot \sum_{i=1}^n r_i f_i^m = \sum_{i=1}^n r_i s_if_i^m = 0\]

    \textbf{SB2:} Say
    \[\Spec(A) = \bigcup_{i \in I}U_{f_i}\]
    and choose elements in each \(A_{f_i}\), which agree on \(A_{f_if_j}\). That is, we have \(s_i \in A_{f_i}\), with the images of \(s_i, s_j\) in \(A_{f_if_j}\) agreeing. We need to build \(s \in A\) with these localisations. 
    
    First suppose \(I\) is finite. On \(A_{f_i}\), we have the element
    \[\frac{a_i}{f_i^{\ell_i}} \in A_{f_i}\]
    We will write \(g_i = f_i^{\ell_i}\), and note that \(U_{f_i} = U_{g_i}\).

    On overlaps, restrict to \(A_{g_ig_j}\). Then we have that
    \[(g_ig_j)^{m_{ij}}(a_i g_j - a_j g_i) = 0\]
    Rewriting this using algebra and the fact that \(U_f = U_{f^k}\) for all \(k\). By taking the largest, we can assume \(m = m_{ij}\). We write
    \[b_i = a_i g_i^m \qquad h_i = g_i^{m+1}\]
    Using this, the element we chose in \(A_{f_i}\), becomes \(b_i/h_i\). But now \(U_{f_i} = U_{h_i}\) cover \(\Spec(A)\), and so we can write
    \[1 = \sum_i r_i h_i\]
    Now we construct
    \[r = \sum r_i b_i\]
    with \(r_i\) as above. This restricts correctly to \(b_i/h_i\) on \(U_{h_i}\) (i.e. in the localisation \(A_{h_i}\)).

    When \(I\) is infinite, choose a finite subcover, or equivalently, \(\inner{f_1, \dots, f_n} = A\), and use the above to build \(r \in A\). But given \(\inner{f_1, \dots, f_n, f_\alpha} = A\), the same construction gives a new element \(r'\). But \(r' = r\) by SB1.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[structure sheaf]
    The \emph{structure sheaf on \(\Spec(A)\)}\index{structure sheaf!of \(\Spec(A)\)} is the sheaf associated to the sheaf on the base sending
    \[U_f \mapsto A_f\]
    The sheaf is denoted \(\mcO_{\Spec(A)}\).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \[\mcO_{\Spec(A), \mfp} = A_\mfp\]
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [ringed space] A \emph{ringed space}\index{ringed space} \((X, \mcO_X)\) is a topological space \(X\), with a sheaf of rings \(\mcO_X\) on \(X\).
\end{definition}

We note that any open subset of a ringed space is naturally a ringed space, with
\[\mcO_U(V) = \mcO_X(V)\]
for all \(V \sse U \sse X\) open.

\begin{definition}
    [isomorphism of ringed spaces]

    An \emph{isomorphism of ringed spaces \((X, \mcO_X) \to (Y, \mcO_Y)\)}\index{isomorphism!of ringed spaces} is
    \begin{itemize}
        \item a homeomorphism \(\pi : X \to Y\),
        \item an isomorphism of sheaves on \(Y\), \(\mcO_Y \to \pi_*\mcO_X\).
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    We could have also chosen \(\pi^{-1}\mcO_Y \to \mcO_X\).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [affine scheme] An \emph{affine scheme}\index{affine scheme} is a ringed space \((X, \mcO_X)\) which is isomorphic to \((\Spec(A), \mcO_{\Spec(A)})\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [scheme] A \emph{scheme}\index{scheme} is a ringed space \((X, \mcO_X)\), that is locally isomorphic to an affine scheme.

    In other words, for every \(x \in X\), there exists \(x \in U \sse X\) open, with \((U, \mcO_U)\) being an affine scheme.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Examples of schemes}

\begin{example}
    \(\Spec(A)\) is a scheme.
\end{example}

\lec

\begin{example}
    [Open subschemes] \index{open subscheme}Let \(X\) be a scheme, \(U \sse X\) an open subset. We write \(i : U \inj X\) for the inclusion map. Using this, we can define
    \[\mcO_U = \mcO_X\vert_U := i^{-1}\mcO_X\]
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    The ringed space \((U, \mcO_U)\) is a scheme.
\end{proposition}

A simple case of this is to take \(X = \Spec(A)\), and the distinguished open \(U = U_f\). Then
\[(U, \mcO_U) \cong (\Spec(A_f), \mcO_{\Spec(A_f)})\]
\begin{proof}
    Let \(p \in U \sse X\). Since \(X\) is a scheme, we can find \(V_p\) such that \(V_p\) is isomorphic to an affine scheme. Take \(V_p \cap U\) with structure sheaf via restriction. Note however \(V_p \cap U\) may not be affine.

    Since \(V_p\) is affine, say \(V_p \cong \Spec(B)\). The distinguished opens form a basis for the Zariski topology on \(\Spec(B)\). So we've reduced to the simple case as above.
\end{proof}

We define \emph{affine space}\index{affine space, \(\aff k n\)}
\[\aff k n = \Spec(k[x_1, \dots, x_n])\]

\begin{example}
    Take \(U = \aff k {n^2} \setminus \{\det(x_{ij}) = 0\}\). That is, ``\(U\) is \(\GL(n, k)\)'' roughly speaking. Eventually we will show multiplication \(U \times U \to U\) is a morphism of schemes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [a non-affine scheme]
    Let \(X = \aff k 2\), \(U = X \setminus \{\inner{x, y}\}\). Roughly speaking, we have \(k^2 \setminus 0\).

    \begin{claim}
        \(U\) is not affine.
    \end{claim}
    \begin{proof}
        Suppose it was. We can compute \(\mcO_U(U)\). Write \(U_x = \aff k 2 \setminus \van(x)\), and define \(U_y = \aff k 2 \setminus \van(y)\). Observe \(U = U_x \cup U_y\), and
        \[U_x \cap U_y = \aff k 2 \setminus \van(xy)\]
        With this,
        \begin{align*}
            \mcO_U(U_x) &= k[x, x^{-1}, y] \\
            \mcO_U(U_y) &= k[x, y, y^{-1}] \\
            \mcO_U(U_x \cap U_y) &= k[x,x^{-1},y,y^{-1}]
        \end{align*}
        and the restriction maps \(\mcO_U(U_x) \to \mcO_U(U_{xy})\) are the obvious ones. By the sheaf axioms,
        \[\mcO_U(U) = k[x, x^{-1}, y] \cap k[x, y, y^{-1}]\]
        where we compute the intersection in \(k[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]\). This means that
        \[\mcO_U(U) = k[x, y]\]
        Contradiction. One way to see this is that in \(U\), there exists a maximal ideal in the global section ring with empty vanishing locuz, namely the maximal ideal \(\inner{x, y}\).
    \end{proof}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    More generally, let \(X\) be a scheme, \(f \in \Gamma(X, \mcO_X) = \mcO_X(X)\). Fix \(p \in X\), then we can consider the stalk \(\mcO_{X,p}\). This is of the form \(A_\mfp\), where \(A\) is a ring and \(\mfp\idealof A\) is a prime ideal. In particular, \(A_\mfp\) has a unique maximal ideal, namely \(\mfp A_\mfp\). We say that \(f\) \emph{vanishes at \(p\)} if its image in \(A_\mfp/\mfp A_\mfp\) is zero. Equivalently, if \(f\) is in \(\mfp A_\mfp\).

    Here, we're using an isomorphism \(V_p\) open to \(\Spec(A)\). For \(f \in \Gamma(X, \mcO_X)\), the set \(\van(f) \sse X\) which is the \emph{vanishing locus} of \(f\) is well defined.
\end{example}

\subsection{Interlude - gluing sheaves}

Let \(X\) be a topological space, with a cover \(\{U_\alpha\}\). Suppose we have sheaves \(\mcF_\alpha\) on \(U_\alpha\), and isomorphisms of sheaves
\[\phi_{\alpha\beta} : \mcF_\alpha\vert_{U_\alpha \cap U_\beta} \to \mcF_\beta\vert_{U_\alpha \cap U_\beta}\]
Such that \(\varphi_{\alpha\alpha} = \id\), \(\varphi_{\alpha\beta} = \varphi_{\beta\alpha}^{-1}\), and the cocycle condition
\[\phi_{\beta\gamma} \circ \phi_{\alpha\beta} = \phi_{\alpha\gamma} \text{ on }U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \cap U_\gamma\]

\begin{proposition}
    We can build a sheaf \(\mcF\) on \(X\). Given \(V \sse X\) open, define
    \[\mcF(V) = \left\{(s_\alpha)_\alpha \mid s_\alpha \in \mcF_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap V) \text{ such that }\phi_{\alpha\beta}(s_\alpha\vert_{V \cap U_\alpha \cap U_\beta}) = s_\beta\vert_{V \cap U_\alpha \cap U_\beta}\right\}\]
    Moreover, \(\mcF\vert_{U_\alpha} = \mcF_\alpha\) on \(U\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    [\(\mcF\) is a presheaf] Given \((s_\alpha) \in \mcF_V\), \(W \sse V\) open, we can take
    \[(s_\alpha)\vert_W = \left(\res^{V \cap U_\alpha}_{W \cap U_\alpha}(s_\alpha)\right)_\alpha\]
    We need to check that this lies in \(\mcF(W)\). But this follows from the sheaf axioms.

    \emph{\(\mcF\) is a sheaf.} The sheaf axioms are clear.

    \emph{Restriction.} We need to build an isomorphism \(\mcF\vert_{U_{\gamma}} \cong \mcF_\gamma\). Define a morphism \(\mcF_\gamma \to \mcF\vert_{U_\gamma}\), where for \(V \sse U_\gamma\), \(s \in \mcF_\gamma(V)\), define the image to be
    \[\left(\phi_{\gamma\alpha}(s\vert_{V \cap U_\alpha})\right)_{\alpha}\]
    We need to check that this is in \(\mcF\vert_{U_\gamma}(V) = \mcF(V)\). But this follows from the cocycle condition, as
    \[\phi_{\alpha\beta}\circ \phi_{\gamma\alpha}(s\vert_{V \cap U_\alpha \cap U_\beta}) = \phi_{\gamma\beta}(s\vert_{V \cap U_\alpha \cap U_\beta})\]
    It is easy to see that this is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\subsection{More schemes}

Let \((X, \mcO_X), (Y, \mcO_Y)\) be schemes, with opens \(U \sse X, V \sse Y\) and an isomorphism \((U, \mcO_X\vert_U) \cong (V, \mcO_Y\vert_V)\) as ringed spaces. We can glue both topological spaces and schemes, that is,
\[S = \frac{X \sqcup Y}{U \sim V}\]
\lec
By definition of the quotient topology, the images of \(X\) and \(Y\) in \(S\) form an open cover, with intersection being the image of \(U\) (or \(V\)). We can then glue the structure sheafs of these open sets as before.

Note that in this case, there is no cocycle condition to check.

\begin{example}
    [bug-eyed line, line with two origin] 
    \label{ex:bug-eyed-line}
    Let \(k\) be a fieodl, and \(X = \Spec(k[t]), Y = \Spec(k[u])\). Set
    \[U = \Spec(k[t, t^{-1}]) = \Spec(k[t]_t) \quad V = \Spec(k[u]_u)\]
    These are distinguised opens. We have natural isomorphism
    \begin{align*}
        U &\to V \\
        t &\mapsfrom u
    \end{align*}
    of rings, which is formally, induced by the above map \(k[u]_u \to k[v]_v\), then apply the contravariant functor.

    On the level of topological spaces, \(X = Y = \aff k 1\), \(U = \aff k 1 \setminus \{\inner{t}\}\), which is \(\aff k 1\) with a point removed. In this case,
    \[\frac{X \sqcup Y}{\sim}\]
    looks like a line with two origins.

    The open sets in the scheme are:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Suppose \(W \sse X \sse S\) or \(W \sse Y \sse S\), these are `nice' open sets. 
        \item If \(W = S \setminus \{p_1, \dots, p_r\}\), where \(p_i \in U \cup V\). The simplest case is when \(W = S\).
        
        What is \(\mcO_S(S)\)? We can use the sheaf axioms to show that \(\mcO_S(S) \cong k[t]\) as above. With this, we see that \(S\) is not an affine scheme.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [projective line]
    Let \(X = \Spec(k[t])\), \(Y = \Spec(k[s])\), \(U = \Spec(k[t, t^{-1}])\) and \(V = \Spec(k[s, s^{-1}])\) as above. We now glue along \(s \mapsto t^{-1}\), and we call the result \(\bb P^1_k\) for the resulting scheme (for now).
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    \(\mcO_{\P^1}(\P^1) = k\)
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The only elements of \(k[t, t^{-1}]\) which are polynomials in \(t\) and \(t^{-1}\) are the constants.
\end{proof}

In particular, \(\P^1\) is not affine.

\begin{example}
    We can similarly build \(S = \aff k 2\) with a doubled origin. This has the property where there exists affine open subschemes \(U_1, U_2 \sse S\), such that \(U_1 \cap U_2\) is not affine.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    [gluing schemes]

    Given schemes \((X_i)_{i \in I}\), open subschemes \(X_{ij} \sse X_i\), with \(X_{ii} = X_i\), isomorphisms \(f_{ij} : X_{ij} \to X_{ji}\), such that \(f_{ii} = \id\), satisfying the cocycle conditions
    \begin{align*}
        f_{ij} &= f_{ji}^{-1} \\
        f_{ik}\vert_{X_{ij\cap X_{ik}}} &= f_{jk}\vert_{X_{ji} \cap X_{jk}} \circ f_{ij}\vert_{X_{ij} \cap X_{ik}}
    \end{align*}
    Then there exists a unique scheme \(X\), with an open cover by \(X\), glued along \(X_{ij} \sim X_{ji}\). 
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}
    [projective space] Let \(A\) be any ring, 
    \[X_i = \Spec\left(A\left[\frac{x_0}{x_i}, \dots, \frac{x_n}{x_i}\right]\right)\]
    and
    \[X_{ij} = X_j \setminus \van\left(\frac{x_j}{x_i}\right)\]
    with isomorphisms
    \begin{align*}
        X_{ij} &\to X_{ji} \\
        \frac{x_k}{x_i} &\mapsto \frac{x_k}{x_j} \cdot \left(\frac{x_i}{x_j}\right)^{-1}
    \end{align*}
    The resulting scheme is called projective \(n\)-space \(\P^n_A\)\index{projective space, \(\P^n_A\)!via gluing}.
\end{example}

\underline{Exercise:} \(\Gamma(\P^n_A, \mcO_{\P^n_A}) = A\).

\subsection{The \(\Proj\) construction}

\begin{definition}
    [\(\Z\)-grading]
    A \emph{\(\Z\)-grading} on a ring \(A\) is a decomposition of
    \[A = \bigoplus_{i \in \Z}A_i\]
    as abelian groups, such that multiplication respects the grading, that is:
    \[A_iA_j \sse A_{i+j}\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Let \(A = k[x_0, \dots, x_n]\), we write \(A_d\) for the set of degree \(d\)-homogeneous polynomials (and \(0\)).

    If \(I \sse k[x_0, \dots, x_n]\) is a homogeneous ideal (i.e. generated by homogeneous elements, of possibly different degrees). Then \(k[x_0, \dots, x_n]/I\) is also naturally graded.
\end{example}

Note by definition \(A_0\) is always a subring. Throughout, we will make the \textbf{assumption} that the degree \(1\) elements generate \(A\) as an \(A_0\)-algebra. That is,
\[A = A_0[A_1]\]
as \(A_0\) algebras.

\lec

Moreover, we will assume \(A_i = 0\) for \(i < 0\). Define
\[A_+ = \bigcup_{i > 0}A_i \sse A\]
for the \emph{irrelevant ideal}\index{irrelevant ideal}.

\begin{definition}
    [homogeneous element, homogeneous ideal] A \emph{homogeneous element}\index{homogeneous!element} is \(f \in A_d\) for some \(d\). An ideal \(I \sse A\) is calle \emph{homogeneous}\index{homogeneous!ideal} if it is generated by homogeneous elements.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [\(\Proj(A)\)]
    The set \(\Proj(A)\) is the set of all homogeneous primes in \(A\), which do not contain \(A_+\).

    If \(I \idealof A\) is homogeneous, then we can define
    \[\van(I) = \left\{\mfp \in \Proj(A) \mid I \sse \mfp\right\}\]
    We can define the Zariski topology on \(\Proj(A)\) to have closed sets \(\van(I)\) where \(I\) is homogeneous.
\end{definition}

Let \(f \in A_1\) and \(U_f = \Proj(A) \setminus \van(f)\). Then observe that \(\{U_f\}_{f \in A_1}\) covers \(\Proj(A)\), since by assumption, elements of \(A_1 \sse A\) generate \(\inner{1} \idealof A\).

The ring \(A[1/f] = A_f\) is naturally \(\Z\)-graded, by saying \(\deg(f^{-1}) = -\deg(f)\).

\begin{example}
    Let \(A = k[x_0, x_1], f = x_0\), then in
    \[A[1/f] = k[x_0, x_1, x_0^{-1}]\]
    we have degree zero elements \(\lambda \in k\), but also
    \[\frac{x_1^d}{x_0^d} \text{ for all }d \ge 0\]
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    There is a natural bijection
    \[\left(\{\text{homog. primes in }A_f\} \bij\right) \{\text{homog. primes in }A\text{ which miss f}\} \bij \{\text{primes in }(A_f)_{\deg=0}\}\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof/Construction]
    
    First, observe that homogeneous primes in \(A\) missing \(f\) are naturally in bijection with homogeneous primes in \(A_f\). Suppose \(\mfq \sse (A_f)_{\deg = 0}\) is a prime. Then let \(\Psi(\mfq)\) be the ideal generated by
    \[\bigcup_{d \ge 0}\left\{a \in A_d \ \bigg\vert\ \frac{a}{f^d} \in \mfq\right\} \sse A\]
    \(\Psi(\mfq)\) is a prime. Conversely, let \(\mfp \sse A\) be a homogeneous prime missing \(f\), take
    \[\varphi(\mfp) = \mfp \cdot A[1/f] \cap (A[1/f])_{\deg = 0}\]

    Note that the first part is the contraction, and the second part is the extension of ideals with respect to localisation (almost).

    In particular, \(\varphi \circ \Psi = \id\). For \(\Psi \circ \varphi = \id\), we will prove \(\mfp = \Psi(\varphi(\mfp))\) by showing both containments.

    Suppose \(\mfp \in U_f \sse \Proj(A)\), if \(a \in \mfp \cap A_d\), then (we can assume without loss of generality\footnote{We can write \(a\) as a \emph{sum} of such elements.})
    \[\frac{a}{f^d} \in \varphi(\mfp)\]
    and so \(a \in \Psi(\varphi(\mfp))\). Conversely, if \(a \in \Psi(\varphi(\mfp))\), then
    \[\frac{a}{f^d} \in \varphi(\mfp)\]
    for some \(d\). So there exists \(b \in \mfp\), such that
    \[\frac{b}{f^e} = \frac{a}{f^d}\]
    But then
    \[f^k(f^d b - f^e a) = 0\]
    By primality, \(f^{e + k} \notin \mfp\), and so \(a \in \mfp\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    The bijection we constructed above is order preserving. That is, it defines a homeomorphism
    \[U_f \to \Spec\left((A_f)_{\deg = 0}\right)\]
\end{remark}
That is, \(\Proj(A)\) is covered by open sets, each homeomorphic to \(\Spec((A_f)_{\deg = 0})\) for some \(f\). If \(f, g \in A_1\), then \(U_f \cap U_g\) is naturally homeomorphic to
\[\Spec\left((A[1/f])_{\deg = 0}[f/g]\right) = \Spec((A[f^{-1}, g^{-1}])_{\deg = 0})\]
Take the open cover \(\{U_f\}\), withj structure sheaf \(\mcO_{(\Spec(A_f))_{\deg = 0}}\) on each \(U_f\), and we have isomorphisms on \(U_f \cap U_g\) given by the above. The cocycle condition follows from the properties of localisation, and so \(\Proj(A)\) is a scheme.

\begin{definition}
    [projective space]
    Let \(A = k[x_0, \dots, x_n]\) with the standard grading, then we denote
    \[\P^n_k = \Proj(A)\]
    for the \emph{projective space}\index{projective space, \(\P^n_A\)!as \(\Proj\) of a ring}.
\end{definition}

We will see that this is the same as the gluing construction of projective space, but we don't have a notion of ``same'' yet.

\section{Morphisms}

We have seen some maps, which \emph{should} be morphisms. For example, for \(U \sse X\) an open subscheme, \(U \inj X\). Or if \(f : A \to B\) is a ring homomorphism, then we should have a morphism of schemes \(\Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\).

\subsection{Morphisms of schemes and locally ringed spaces}

Given a scheme \((X, \mcO_X)\), the stalks \(\mcO_{X, p}\) are local rings, that is there is a unique maximal ideal \(\mfm_{X, p}\). Given a function \(f \in \mcO_X(U)\), \(p \in U\), we can ask: Does \(f\) vanish at \(p\). That is, is \(f_p \in \mfm_{X, p}\)?

\begin{definition}
    [morphism of ringed spaces] A morphism\index{morphism!of ringed spaces} \(f : (X, \mcO_X) \to (Y, \mcO_Y)\) is
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item a continuous map \(f : X \to Y\),
        \item a morphism of sheaves of rings on \(Y\), \(f^\sharp : \mcO_Y \to f_*\mcO_X\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\lec

\underline{Warning:} It is possible to find \((f, f^\sharp)\) a morphism between schemes \((X, \mcO_X), (Y, \mcO_Y)\), with \(U \sse Y\) open, \(q \in U\), \(h \in \mcO_Y(U)\), such that \(h\) vanishes at \(q\), and
\[f^\sharp(h) \in \mcO_X(f^{-1}(U))\]
which does not vanish at \(p \in X\) with \(f(p) = q\).

Observe that: given a morphism \(f : X \to Y\) of ringed spaces, for \(p \in X\), we have an induced map
\[f^\sharp : \mcO_{Y, f(p)} \to \mcO_{X, p}\]
That is, given \(s \in \mcO_{Y, f(p)}\), we can represent it as \((s_U, U)\) where \(U \sse U\) open, \(f(p) \in U\) and \(s_U \in \mcO_Y(U)\).

With this, \(f^\sharp(s_U) \in \mcO_X(f^{-1}(U))\), and so the pair \((f^\sharp(s_U), f^{-1}(U))\) defines an element of \(\mcO_{X, p}\).

\begin{definition}
    [locally ringed space]
    Let \((X, \mcO_X)\) be a ringed space. It is \emph{locally ringed}\index{locally ringed space} if for all \(p \in X\), \(\mcO_{X, p}\) is a local ring. A \emph{morphism of locally ringed spaces}\index{morphism!of locally ringed spaces}
    \[(f, f^\sharp) : (X, \mcO_X) \to (Y, \mcO_Y)\]
    is a morphism of ringed spaces, such that if \(\mfm_p\) denotes the maximal ideal in \(\mcO_{X,p}\) and \(\mfm_{f(p)}\) is the maximal ideal of \(\mcO_{Y, f(p)}\), then
    \[f^\sharp(\mfm_{f(p)}) \sse \mfm_p\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [morphism of schemes] A \emph{morphism of schemes}\index{morphism!of schemes} \(X \to Y\) is a morphism between the locally ringed spaces  \((X, \mcO_X) \to (Y, \mcO_Y)\). 
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    There is a natural bijection
    \[\{\text{morphisms of schemes } \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\} \bij \{\text{ring homomorphisms }A \to B\}\]
\end{theorem}

Recall that a section \(s \in \mcF(U)\) is a coherent collection of elements \(s(p) \in \mcF_p\) for all \(p \in U\).

\begin{proof}
    We'll show that every ring homomorphism induce a morphism of schemes, and every morphism between schemes arises via this construction.

    Given a ring homomorphism \(\varphi : A \to B\), we have an associated continuous map
    \[\widetilde\varphi = \varphi^{-1} : \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\]
    We will now build \(f^\sharp : \mcO_{\Spec(A)} \to \widetilde\varphi_*\mcO_{\Spec(B)}\).

    At stalk level, take the map
    \begin{align*}
        A_{\varphi^{-1}(\mfp)} &\to B_\mfp \\
        \frac{a}{s} &\mapsto \frac{\varphi(a)}{\varphi(s)}
    \end{align*}
    induced by \(\varphi\). Note that if \(s \notin \varphi^{-1}(\mfp)\), then \(\varphi(s) \notin \mfp\). This is automatically local, i.e. it sends the maximal ideal \(\mfm_{f^{-1}(p)} \idealof A_\mfp\) to the maximal ideal \(\mfm_p \idealof B_\mfp\).

    Given \(U \sse \Spec(A)\), we need to define
    \[\varphi^\sharp : \mcO_{\Spec(A)}(U) \to \mcO_{\Spec(B)}(\widetilde\varphi^{-1}(U))\]
    where \(\widetilde\varphi^{-1}(U)\) means take the preimage of \(U\) under \(\varphi^{-1}\). An element \(s \in \mcO_{\Spec(A)}(U)\) is a collection of assignments \((\mfp \mapsto s(\mfp))_{\mfp \in U}\), where \(\mfp \in U, s_\mfp \in A_\mfp\). We define
    \[\varphi^\sharp : \left(s \mapsto s(\mfp)\right)_{\mfp \in U} \mapsto \left(\mfq \mapsto \varphi_\mfq(s(\varphi^{-1}(\mfq)))\right)_{\mfq \in \widetilde\varphi^{-1}(U)}\]
    We can check that this glues.

    Conversely, suppose \((f, f^\sharp) : \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\) is a morphism of schemes. Using the fact that we have a ring homomorphism
    \[A = \mcO_{\Spec(A)}(\Spec(A)) \to \mcO_{\Spec(B)}(\Spec(B)) = B\]
    we get a ring homomorphism \(g : A \to B\). We need to check that \(\widetilde g = f : \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\), and the construction from the first part gives the correct map on strcture sheaves.

    The maps on stalks are compatible with restriction. That is, the diagram

\subsection{Housekeeping}

\begin{definition}
    [open immersion, closed immersion]
    Let \(X, Y\) be schemes. A morphism of schemes \(f : X \to Y\) is an \emph{open immersion}\index{open immersion} if \(f\) induces an isomorphism of \(X\) onto an open subscheme of \(Y\), \((U, \mcO_Y\vert_U)\) where \(U \sse Y\) is open.

    A morphism \(g : X \to Y\) is a \emph{closed immersion}\index{closed immersion} if the map on topological space is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset of \(Y\), and \(\mfg^\sharp : \mcO_Y \to g_*\mcO_X\) is surjective.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Consider the ring homomorphism
    \[k[x] \mapsto \frac{k[t]}{t^2}\]
    Taking spectra, we have a closed immersion.
\end{example}

\subsection{Fibre products}

\begin{itemize}

\begin{theorem}
    Fibre product of schemes exist.
\end{theorem}

For full details, see Hartshorne Chapter 2 Theorem 3.3.

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{Step 1: Let \(X, Y, S\) be affine schemes.} Say \(X = \Spec(A), Y = \Spec(B), S = \Spec(R)\). Then the fibre product \(X\times_S Y\) exists, and it is isomorphic to
    \[\Spec(A \otimes_R B)\]
    That is, we will check that the universal property is satisfied. That is, given any scheme \(Z\) with morphisms
    % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNCxbMiwyLCJTIl0sWzAsMiwiWSJdLFsyLDAsIlgiXSxbMCwwLCJaIl0sWzMsMl0sWzMsMV0sWzEsMF0sWzIsMF1d
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	Z && X \\
	\\
	Y && S
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=1-3]
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=3-1]
	\arrow[from=3-1, to=3-3]
	\arrow[from=1-3, to=3-3]
\end{tikzcd}\]
    there exists a unique morphism \(Z \to \Spec(A \otimes_R B)\). If \(Z\) is affine, then it is clear, using the corresponding morphisms of rings, and the universal property of tensor products.

    \underline{Fact (Examples sheet 2):} A scheme theoretic map \(Z \to \Spec(A \otimes_R B)\) is the same data as \(A \otimes_R B \to \Gamma(Z, \mcO_Z)\).

    \textbf{Step 2: Now let \(X, Y, S\) be any schemes.} If \(X \times_S Y\) exists, \(U \sse X\) is an open subscheme, then \(U \times_S Y\) also exists. To see this, take the inverse image of \(U\) under the projection \(X \times_Y S \to X\), with the open subscheme structure.

    If \(X\) is covered by opens \(X_i\), if \(X_i \times_S Y\) exists for all \(i\), then \(X \times_S Y\) exists. This is because the schemes will glue together. Note that in this case there are no cocycle conditions.

    \textbf{Step 3: Let \(X\) be any scheme, \(S, Y\) are affine}, then by steps 1 and 2, \(X \times_S Y\) exists.

    \textbf{Step 4: Let \(X, Y\) be any scheme, \(S\) is affine}. This is because we can exchange \(X\) and \(Y\) in the above.

    \textbf{Step 5: Let \(X, Y, S\) be any schemes.} Say \(S\) is covered by affines \(S_i\). Let \(X_i, Y_i\) be the preimages of \(S_i\) in \(X\) and \(Y\) respectively. Since the \(S_i\) are affine, \(X_i \times_{S_i}Y_i\) exists. By the universal property, \(X_i \times_{S_i} Y_i = X_i \times_S Y_i\). Finally, we glue these together to get \(X \times_S Y\).
\end{proof}

    Suppose \(f : X \to S\) is a closed map (topologically). It is \emph{universally closed}\index{universally closed} if for any \(S' \to S\), the induced map \(X \times_S S' \to S'\) is also closed.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [proper] We say that \(f\) is \emph{proper}\index{proper} if it is separated, finite type and universally closed.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    [check/wait]
    Closed immersions are proper.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    Let \(R\) be any ring, then the map \(\P^n_R \to \Spec(R)\) is proper.
\end{proposition}

Observe that a morphism \(X \to S\) being universally closed is stable under base extension. Since we already saw \(\P^n_R \to \Spec(R)\) is separated, and finite type is clear. Hence all we need to check is the case \(R = \Z\), since
\[\P^n_R = \P^n_\Z \times_{\Spec(\Z)} \Spec(R)\] 

\begin{proof}
    We must show that for any \(Y \to \Spec(Z)\), the base extension \(\P^n_\Z \times_{\Spec(\Z)} Y \to Y\) is closed. But \(Y\) is covered by affine schemes of the form \(\Spec(R)\), and closedness is local on the target, it suffices to show \(\P^n_R \to \Spec(R)\) is closed. Let \(Z \sse \P^n_R\) be Zariski closed. That is, \(Z = \van(g_1, \dots)\) of homogeneous polynomials \(g_i\). If \(\pi : \P^n_R \to \Spec(R)\) is the map, then we would like to show that \(\pi(Z)\) is a closed set in \(\Spec(R)\).

    That is, we need equations for \(\pi(Z)\). Equvialently, we need to characterise those primes \(\mfp \idealof R\) such that \(\pi^{-1}(\mfp) \cap Z\) is non-empty.

    Let \(K(\mfp) = \Frac(R/\mfp)\), and then we have a morphism \(\Spec(K(\mfp)) \to \Spec(R)\). We would like to know for which \(\mfp\) is \(Z_\mfp = Z \times_{\Spec(R)} \Spec(K(\mfp))\) non-empty.

    What is \(Z_\mfp\)? We take the equations \(g_1, g_2, \dots\), which are homogeneous polynomials with coefficients in \(R\). Reducing \(\mod \mfp\), we get \(\overline{\mfg_1}, \overline{\mfg_2}, \dots\), which has coefficients in \(K(\mfp)\).

    So \(Z_\mfp\) is non-empty if and only if \(\overline{\mfg_1}, \overline{\mfg_2}, \dots\) cut out more than origin in \(\aff{K(\mfp)}{n+1}\). Thus,
    \[Z_\mfp \text{ is non-empty } \iff \sqrt{\inner{\overline{g_1}, \overline{g_2}, \dots}}\not\supseteq \inner{x_0, \dots, x_n}\]
    where \(\P^n_R = \Spec(R[x_0, \dots, x_n])\).

    Equivalently, for all positive integers \(d\),
    \[\inner{x_0, \dots, x_n}^d \not\subseteq \pinner{\overline{g_1}, \overline{g_2}, \dots}\]
    Write \(A = R[x_0, \dots, x_n]\) with the usual grading. Non-containment is equivalent to the map
    \[\bigoplus_i A_{d - \deg(g_i)} \to A_d\]
    given by
    \[(f_i)_i \mapsto \sum_i f_ig_i\]
    being non-surjective \(\mathrm{mod } \mfp\) (equivalently in \(K(\mfp)\)), for all \(d\). The condition is given by vanishing of maximal minors of the matrix associated to the above map, which is infinitely many polynomials in the \(g_i\).
\end{proof}

From now on, all schemes will be assumed to be Noetherian. That is, it has a finite cover by open subschemes of the form \(\Spec(R)\), where \(R\) is a Noetherian ring.

\subsection{Valuative criteria (for separatedness and properness)}

Recall a \emph{discete valuation ring}\index{discrete valuation ring} is a local PID\footnote{As we are assuming the ring is Noetherian.}.

\begin{example}
    \(\C\powers t\) is a DVR, and so is
    \[\mcO_{\mathbb A^1, 0} = \left\{\frac{f(t)}{g(t)} \ \bigg\vert\ g(0) \ne 0\right\}\]
    Moreover, so are \(\Z_{\inner{\mfp}}\) (localisation) and \(\Z_p\) (\(p\)-adic integers). 
\end{example}

Let \(A\) be a discrete valuation ring, then \(\Spec(A)\) consists of two points, \(0 \idealof A\) and the maximal ideal \(\mfm \idealof A\). The topology on \(\Spec(A)\) has:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\{0\}\) is dense,
    \item \(\mfm\) is closed.
\end{itemize}
Any generator \(\pi\) of \(\mfm\) is called a \emph{uniformiser}\index{uniformiser} or a \emph{uniformising parameter}\index{uniformising parameter}.

In \(\C\powers t\), \(\inner{t}\) is a maximal ideal, and the units are power series with non-zero constant coefficient. Intuitively, \(\C[t]\) is the line, \(\C\powers t\) is the ``germ of the curve at \(0\)''.

Any element \(a \in A\) can be written as \(u\pi^k\), where \(u \in A\) is a unit, \(k\) is unique. The integer \(k\) is called the \emph{valuation of \(a\)}\index{valuation}. This gives a map
\[\val : A \setminus 0 \to \N\]

\lec

\(K = \Frac(A)\) is a \emph{valued field}\index{valued field} and \(\val\) extends to a map
\[\val : K^\times \to \Z\]
with \(\val(a/b) = \val(a) - \val(b)\).

If we take \(A = k\powers t\), then \(K = k\laur t\), and the valuation of
\[a_\ell t^\ell + a_{\ell+1}t^{\ell+1} + \cdots\]
with \(a_\ell \ne 0\) is \(\ell\).

In this case, we have an open immersion \(\Spec(K) \to \Spec(A)\). As an analogy, we can think of \(\Spec(K)\) as the punctured unit disc, and \(\Spec(A)\) as the unit disc. Intuitively, if we map \(\Spec(K)\) to a ``compact'' space, we can extend it to \(\Spec(A)\) by ``filling in the origin''.

\section{Modules over \(\mcO_X\)}

\subsection{Motivation}

\begin{example}
    [please forget all scheme theory] Consider the variety
    \[\C \mathrm{P}^n = \frac{\C^{n+1}\setminus 0}{\C^*}\]
    On this, we have a structure sheaf \(\mcO_{\C \mathrm{P}^n}\), and if \(U \sse \C \mathrm{P}^n\) is Zariski open, then
    \[\mcO_{\C\mathrm{P}^n}(U) = \left\{\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \ \bigg\vert\ P, Q \text{ homogenous of the same degree}, P/Q\text{ regular on U}\right\}\]
    For any integer \(d\), we can consider the sheaf of abelian groups \(\mcO_{\C\mathrm P^n}(d)\), given by
    \[\mcO_{\C\mathrm P^n}(d)(U) = \left\{\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \ \bigg\vert\ P, Q \text{ homogenous with }\deg(P) - \deg(Q) = d, P/Q \text{ regular on U}\right\}\]
    In fact, \(\mcO_{\C\mathrm P^n}(d)(U)\) is an \(\mcO_{\C\mathrm P^n}(U)\) module in the natural way.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [please remember all of scheme theory] Let \(A\) be a ring, \(M\) an \(A\)-module. Define a sheaf \(\mcF_M\) on \(\Spec(A)\) of abelian groups, if \(U_f \sse \Spec(A)\) is a distinguished open, then we can set
    \[\mcF_M(U) = M_f\]
    which is the localisation. On general opens, use sheaf on a base construction.
\end{example}

Another way to think about this as the Algebraic Geometry analogue of vector bundles.

\subsection{Definitions}

Fix a ringed space \((X, \mcO_X)\).

\begin{definition}
    [sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-modules] A \emph{sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-modules}\index{sheaf!of \(\mcO_X\)-modules} is a sheaf \(\mcF\) of groups, along with a map \(\mcO_X(U) \to \mcF(U) \to \mcF(U)\) making \(\mcF(U)\) into an \(\mcO_X(U)\)-module. Moreover, we require this to be compatible with restrictions, i.e.
    \[(r \cdot m)\vert_V = r\vert_V \cdot m\vert_V\]
\end{definition}

Similarly, we can define a sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-algebras. A \emph{morphism between sheaves of \(\mcO_X\)-modules}\index{morphism!of sheaves of \(\mcO_X\)-modules} is defined in the usual way, that is, a morphism between sheaves of abelian groups compatible with the \(\mcO_X\)-module structure.

\lec

\begin{example}
    [sheaf associated to a module]
    \index{sheaf!associated to a module}
    If \(X = \Spec(A), M\) an \(A\)-module, then we have a sheaf \(M^{\sh}\) on \(X\), such that
    \[M^{\sh}(U_f) = M_f\]
    and we extend it to all opens.
\end{example}

We have basic operations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item give a morphism \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\) of (sheaves of) \(\mcO_X\)-modules, we have \(\ker(f)\), \(\coker(f)\), \(\im(f)\),
    \item we can take direct sums, direct products, tensor product, \(\Hom\)s,
\end{itemize}
which extend in the ``natural way''. Note \(\coker, \im\) and tensor product requires sheafification.

The sheaf tensor product \(\mcF \otimes_{\mcO_X}\mcG\) has
\[(\mcF \otimes_{\mcO_X} \mcG)(U) = \mcF(U) \otimes_{\mcO_X(U)} \mcG(U)\]
and we can then sheafify this.

If \(f : X \to Y\) is a morphism of ringed spaces (or schemes), and given an \(\mcO_X\)-module \(\mcF\), the \emph{pushforward}\index{pushforward!of a sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-modules} \(f_*\mcF\) is a sheaf of abelian groups, but we have a map \(f^\sharp : \mcO_Y \to f_*\mcO_X\). This gives \(f_*\mcF\) and \(\mcO_Y\)-module structure.

Given \(U \sse Y\) open, \(a \in \mcO_Y(U)\), \(m \in f_*\mcF(U) = \mcF(f^{-1}(U))\), we define
\[am := f^\sharp(a)m\]
where we note that \(f^\sharp(a) \in \mcO_X(f^{-1}(U))\). Conversely, if \(\mcG\) is a sheaf of \(\mcO_Y\)-modules, then we define the \emph{pullback}\index{pullback!of a sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-modules} sheaf
\[f^*\mcG = f^{-1}\mcG \otimes_{f^{-1}\mcO_y}\mcO_X\]
where the \(f^{-1}\mcO_Y\)-module structure on \(\mcO_X\) is defined via the adjoint to \(f^\#\). See examples sheet 1 Q14.

\subsection{\(\mcO_X\)-modules on schemes and quasi-coherence}

\begin{definition}
    [(quasi-)coherent sheaf]
    A \emph{quasi-coherent sheaf \(\mcF\) (on a scheme of \(X\))}\index{quasi-coherent sheaf} of \(\mcO_X\)-modules is a sheaf of \(\mcO_X\)-modules \(\mcF\), such that there exists a cover of \(X\) by affines \(U_i\), such that \(\mcF\vert_{U_i}\) is the sheaf associated to a module over the ring \(\mcO_X(U_i)\).

    If the module over the \(\mcO_X(U_i)\) can be taken to be finitely generated, we say that \(\mcF\) is \emph{coherent}\index{coherent sheaf}\footnote{Recall we assumed our schemes are Noetherian.}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    On any scheme, \(\mcO_X\) is quasi-coherent (in fact coherent). More generally, \(\mcO_X^{\oplus n}\) is coherent. On the other hand, \(\mcO_X^{\oplus I}\) is quasi-coherent but not coherent if \(I\) is infinite.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    If \(i : X \inj Y\) is a closed module, then \(i_*\mcO_X\) is a quasi-coherent \(\mcO_Y\)-module. Say \(U = \Spec(A) \sse Y\) is affine, then \(X \cap U \inj U\) gives an ideal \(I \idealof A\), which is the kernel of the map on structure sheafs \(\mcO_Y(U) \surj \mcO_X(X \cap U)\). On \(U\), \(i_*\mcO_X\vert_U\) is the sheaf associated to the \(A\)-module \(A/I\).
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:quasi-coherent-iff}
    An \(\mcO_X\)-module \(\mcF\) is quasi-coherent if and only if for any open affine \(U = \Spec(A) \sse X\), \(\mcF\vert_U\) is the sheaf associated to a module over \(A\).

    Similarly, \(\mcF\) is coherent if and only if each \(\mcF\vert_U\) is finitely generated as an \(A\)-module.
\end{proposition}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:restriction-of-global-section-if-mult}
    Let \(X = \Spec(A)\) be a scheme, \(f \in A\), \(\mcF\) a quasi-coherent \(\mcO_X\)-module. Let \(s \in \Gamma(X, \mcF)\). Then
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item if \(s\) restricts to zero on \(U_f\), then \(f^ns = 0\) for some \(n\).
        \item if \(t \in \mcF(U_f)\), then for some \(n\), \(f^n t\) is the restriction of a global section of \(\mcF\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    There exists some cover of \(X\) by affine opens \(V = \Spec(B)\), such that
    \[\mcF_V = M^{\sh}\]
    for a \(B\)-module \(M\). But we can cover \(V\) by distinguished affines of the form \(U_g\) for some \(g \in A\). In this case,
    \[\mcF\vert_{U_g} = \left(M \otimes_B A_g\right)^{\sh}\]
    since \(\mcF\vert_V\) is already quasi-coherent. But recall that \(\Spec(A)\) is quasi-compact, i.e. every open cover has a finite subcover, and so finitely many \(g_i, U_{g_i}\) and \(M_i\) will suffice to cover \(X\) by open such that
    \[\mcF\vert_{U_{g_i}} = M_i^{\sh}\]
    Result then follows from formal properties of localisation. See Hartshorne for details.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{prop:quasi-coherent-iff}] Let \(\mcF\) be a quasicoherent sheaf on \(X\). Given \(U \sse X\) open, \(\mcF\vert_U\) is also quasi-coherent. Hence we can reduce to the case when \(X = \Spec(A)\). Take \(M = \mcF(X)\), and \(M^{\sh}\) the associated sheaf. We claim that \(M^{\sh} \cong F\).

    Let \(\alpha : M^{\sh} \to \mcF\) be the map given by restricting global sections (e.g. via stalks). Moreover, \(\alpha\) is an isomorphism at stalk level. But this is just the lemma.
\end{proof}

\lec

\textbf{Facts} (proofs omitted and so non-examinable):
\begin{itemize}
    \item images, kernels and cokernels of maps of quasi-coherent (resp. coherent) sheaves remain quasi-coherent (resp. coherent).
    \item if \(f : X \to S\) is a morphism of schemes, \(\mcF\) on \(S\) is quasi-coherent (resp. coherent). Then \(f^*\mcF\) is quasi-coherent (resp. coherent).
    \item if \(f : X \to S\) is a morphism of schemes, \(\mcG\) a quasi-coherent sheaf on \(X\), then \(f_*\mcG\) is quasi-coherent on \(S\). In general, if \(\mcG\) is coherent, then \(f_*\mcF\) need not be coherent. For example, take the natural map \(\aff k 1 \to \Spec(k)\), then \(f_*\mcO_{\aff k 1}\) is a quasi-coherent sheaf on \(\Spec(k)\), i.e. a \(k\)-vector space. In particular, it is \(k[x]\), which is \emph{not} a finite dimensional \(k\)-vector space.
    
    Observe if we took \(\P^1 \to \Spec(k)\) instead, then \(f_*\mcO_{\P^1}\) is just the sheaf associated to \(k\).

    More generally, if \(\mcG\) is a coherent sheaf on \(X\), \(f : X \to S\) is proper, then \(f_*\mcF\) is cohereent. We will prove this for closed immersions on examples sheet 3.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Source of examples:} Let \(A\) be an \(\N\)-graded ring (with the usual assumptions), we built \(\Proj(A)\) which is a scheme. This was covered by \(\Spec(A[1/f]_0)\) for \(f \in A_1\).

\begin{definition}
    Let \(M\) be a graded \(A\)-module, that is,
    \[M = \bigoplus_{d \in \Z}M_d\]
    where \(M_d\) is an abelian group. \(M\) is an \(A\)-module, and \(A_i M_j \sse M_{i+j}\). Consider the sheaf determined by the association
    \[\Proj(A) \supseteq U_f \mapsto M[1/f]_0\]
    That is, the degree zero part of localisation of \(M\) at \(f\). This gives a quasi-coherent sheaf on \(\Proj(A)\), by the same arguments as in the construction of \(\Proj(A)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{notation}
    Let \(X\) be a scheme, \(\mcF\) a quasi-coherent \(\mcO_X\)-module. We say
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\mcF\) is \emph{free}\index{free} if \(\mcF \cong \mcO_X^{\bigoplus I}\) for some indexing set \(I\). Note these are coherent if and only if the indexing set \(I\) is finite.
        \item \(\mcF\) is called a \emph{(algebraic) vector bundle}\index{vector bundle} if there exists an open cover \(\{U_i\}\), such that \(\mcF\vert_{U_i}\) is free. This is also known as \emph{locally free}.
        \item A \emph{line bundle}\index{line bundle} or an \emph{invertible sheaf} is a vector bundle which is locally isomorphic to \(\mcO_X\).
    \end{itemize}
\end{notation}

\subsection{Coherent sheaves on projective space}

\begin{definition}
    Let \(A\) be a graded ring, \(M\) a graded \(A\)-module. Let \(d\) be an integer, and define the \emph{twisting}\index{twisting} \(M(d)\) for the module such that
    \[M(d)_k = M_{k + d}\]

    Let \(X = \Proj(A)\), then the sheaf \(\mcO_X(d)\) is the sheaf associated to the graded module \(A(d)\) for \(d \in \Z\). We will call \(\mcO_X(1)\) the \emph{twisting sheaf}\index{twisting sheaf}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \(\mcO_X(d) = \mcO_X(1)^{\otimes d}\). This follows from the fact that tensor product on graded modules acts additively on the grading.
\end{remark}

Let \(X = \Proj(k[x_1, \dots, x_n]) = \P^n_k\). Then global sections of \(\mcO_{\P^n}(d)\) are naturally identified with homogeneous degree \(d\) polynomials in the \(x_i\). In particular, if \(d < 0\), then there are no non-zero global sections of \(\mcO_{\P^n}(d)\).

\begin{definition}
    [globally generated]
    An \(\mcO_X\)-module \(\mcF\) is called \emph{globally generated}\index{globally generated} or \emph{generated by global sections} if it is a quotient of \(\mcO_X^r\) for some \(r\). That is, if there exists a surjective map \(\mcO_X^r \to \mcF\).

    Equivalently, there exists \(s_1, \dots, s_r \in \mcF(X)\), such that the \(s_i\) generate the stalks \(\mcF_p\) over \(\mcO_{X, p}\) for all \(p\).
\end{definition}

Let \(i : X \to \P^n_R\) be a closed immersion. \(\mcO_X(1)\) be the restriction of \(\mcO_{\P^n}(1)\) to \(X\). That is, \(\mcO_X(1) = i^*\mcO_{\P^n}(1)\).

\begin{theorem}
    [Serre]
    Let \(\mcF\) be a coherent sheaf on \(X\). Then there exists \(d_0 \in \Z\), such that for all \(d \ge d_0\), the sheaf
    \[\mcF(d) = \mcF \otimes_{\mcO_X} \mcO_X(d)\]
    is globally generated.
\end{theorem}

In particular, every \(\mcF\) as above is a quotient of a vector bundle.

\begin{proof}
    By formal properties, it is equivalent to show the statement for \(i_*\mcF\). That is, \(i_*\mcF(d)\) is globally generated on \(\P^n_R\).

    \textbf{Strategy:} First we will cover by affines \(U_i = \Spec(R[x_0/x_i, \dots, x_n/x_i])\). Then \(i_*\mcF\vert_{U_i}\) is a sheaf associated to a module \(M_i\). Choose generators \(\{s_{ij}\}\) for \(M_i\). Finally, we will clear denominators by multiplying by \(x_i^d\) for some large \(d\), and extend them to generators of global sections of \(\mcF(d)\).

\lec

    Write \(\P^n_R = \Proj(R[x_0, \dots, x_n])\), and cover \(\P^n_R\) by \(U_i\), where
    \[U_i = \Spec\left(R\left[\frac{x_0}{x_i}, \dots, \frac{x_n}{x_i}\right]\right)\]
    Now \(\mcF\vert_{U_i} = M_i^{\sh}\), where \(M_i\) is a finitely generated \(R_i\)-module.

    Choose generators \(\{s_{ij}\}\) for \(M_i\).

    \begin{claim}
        The sections \(\{x_i^ds_{ij}\}_{j}\) of \(\mcF(d)(U_i) = (\mcF \otimes \mcO(d))(U_i)\) are restrictions of global sections \(t_{ij} \in \Gamma(\P^n, \mcF(d))\) for all sufficiently large \(d\).
    \end{claim}

    \begin{proof}
        Left as an exercise. Say \(s_{ij} \in M_i = \mcF_i(U_i)\), and let \(x_i \in \mcO(1)\). We claim that \(x_i^ds_{ij} \in (\mcF\otimes \mcO(d))\vert_{U_i}\) is the restriction of a global section.

        In the case when \(X = \P_1\), we can cover \(U_1 = \P^1\setminus \{0\}\) and \(U_2 = \P^2 \setminus \{\infty\}\). Restrict \(s_{1j}\) to \(U_1 \cap U_2\). By \cref{lem:restriction-of-global-section-if-mult}, this is a restriction after multiplying by a high power of \(x_i\).
    \end{proof}

    On \(U_i\), \(s_{ij}\) gobally generate \(M_i^{\sh}\), but we have a morphism of sheaves
    \begin{align*}
        \cdot x_i^d : \mcF &\to \mcF(d) \\
        s &\mapsto s \otimes x_i^d = x_i^d s 
    \end{align*}
    On each \(U_i\), this restricts to an isomorphism for \(\mcF\vert_{U_i} \to \mcF(d)\vert_{U_i}\), since \(x_i\) is invertible on \(U_i\). Since the \(s_{ij}\) generate \(\mcF\vert_{U_i}\), the \(x_i^ds_{ij}\) generate \(\mcF(d)\vert_{U_i}\). With this, the \(t_{ij}\) globally generate.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    With the notation as above, \(\mcF\) is a quotient of \(\mcO(-d)^{\oplus N}\) for some sufficiently large \(N\), \(d \in \Z\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    In the theorem, we have \(\mcO_X^{\oplus N} \to \mcF(d)\). Now tensor with \(\mcO(-d)\).
\end{proof}

\section{Divisors}

In rings, we have two classes of special ideals. Principal (prime) ideals and height \(1\) prime ideals. Recall if \(\mfp \in \Spec(R)\), the height of \(\mfp\), \(\height(\mfp)\) is the largest \(n\) such that there exists a chain of inclusions
\[\mfp_0 \subsetneq \mfp_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \mfp_n = \mfp\]
In particular, if \(R\) is an integral domain, \(\mfp \in \Spec(R)\) has height \(1\) if and only if there is no non-zero prime strictly contained in \(\mfp\).

\begin{example}
    In \(\C[x, y]\), then \(\inner{0}\) has height 0, \(\inner{x}\) has height \(1\), \(\inner{xy}\) has height \(2\).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    In a UFD, height \(1\) prime and principal prime ideals are the same.
\end{remark}

We will now globalise both notions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item height \(1\) primes correspond to Weil divisors.
    \item principal ideals correspond to Cartier divisors.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}
    [generic point]
    If \(X\) is an integral scheme, \(U = \Spec(A)\) is an open affine in \(X\), then the ideal \(0 \in \Spec(A)\) is called the \emph{generic point}\index{generic point} of \(X\). This is true for any \(U\) open affine. We denote this as \(\eta\) or \(\eta_X\).
\end{definition}

This is well defined since any two affine opens intersect, by irreducibility (integral schemes are irreducible), and the generic point is unique as we can consider the gluing.

In this case, \(\mcO_{X, \eta_X} = \Frac(A)\) is a field, and this is independent of the choice of \(A\). We denote this as \(k(X)\), the \emph{function field}\index{function field} of \(X\).

\subsection{Topological facts}

\begin{definition}
    [dimension, codimension] For a topological space \(X\), the \emph{dimension}\index{dimension} of \(X\) is the length of the longest chain of nonempty closed irreducible subsets
    \[Z_0 \subsetneq Z_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n\]
    in \(X\).

    Let \(Z \sse X\) be closed and irreducible. The \emph{codimension}\index{codimension} of \(Z\) in \(X\) is the longest chain
    \[Z = Z_0 \subsetneq Z_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    If \(X\) is a Noetherian topological space, then every closed \(Z \sse X\) has a decomposition into finitely many irreducible closed subsets.
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}
    [regular in codimension \(1\)]
    Let \(X\) be a Noetherian integral separated scheme, then \(X\) is \emph{regular in codimension \(1\)}\index{regular in codimension \(1\)} if for all \(Y \sse X\) closed irreducible codimension \(1\), let \(\eta_Y\) denote the generic point of \(Y\), then \(\mcO_{X, \eta_Y}\) is a discrete valuation ring (i.e. a local PID).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Weil divisors}

Assume \(X\) is a (Noetherian) integral separated and regular in codimension \(1\) scheme.

\begin{definition}
    [prime divisor, Weil divisor]
    A \emph{prime divisor}\index{prime divisor} on \(X\) is an integral closed subscheme of codimension \(1\). A \emph{Weil divisor}\index{Weil divisor} is an element of the free abelian group \(\Div(X)\) generated by the prime divisors.
\end{definition}

We will write \(D \in \Div(X)\) as
\[D = \sum_i n_{Y_i}[Y_i]\]
where \(Y_i\) are prime. A Weil divisor \(D\) is \emph{effective}\index{effective} if all \(n_{Y_i} \ge 0\).

\begin{proposition}
    Let \(f \in \mcO_{X, \eta_X} = k(X)\). For every \(Y \sse X\) a prime divisor, the ring \(\mcO_{X, \eta_Y}\) is a DVR\footnote{as \(X\) is regular in codimension \(1\)}, and we can calculate the valuation \(\nu_Y(f)\) of \(f\) in the DVR. We define the \emph{divisor}\index{divisor!of a function}
    \[\div(f) = \sum_{Y \sse X \text{ prime Weil divisor}}\nu_Y(f)[Y]\]
    
    Then \(\div(f)\) is a Weil divisor.
\end{proposition}

First, if \(X\) is integral, choose \(U \sse X\), \(U = \Spec(A)\), then \(\mcO_{X,\eta} = \Frac(A)\). Since \(\eta\) is contained in every open affine, \(\mcO_{X, \eta}\) allows arbitraty denominators.

\lec

\begin{proof}
    We just need to check that the sum is finite. Let \(f \in k(X)^\times\), and choose \(A\) such that \(U = \Spec(A)\) is an affine open (so \(k(X) = \Frac(A)\)), and \(f \in A\). We can assume this by localising at the denominators. Geometrically, \(f\) is regular on \(U\).

    In this case, \(X \setminus U\) is closed of codimension at least \(1\), and so we have only finitely many prime divisors of \(X\), which are contained in \(X \setminus U\). On \(U\), \(f\) is regular, i.e. \(\nu_Y(f)\ge 0\). But \(\nu_Y(f) > 0\) if and only if \(Y \sse \van(f) \sse U\). By the same argument, only finitely many \(Y\) are contained in \(\van(f)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    [principal divisor]
    A Weil divisor of the form \(\div(f)\) is called \emph{principal}\index{principal}. In \(\Div(X)\), the set of principal divisors form a subgroup \(\Prin(f)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [(Weil divisor) class group]
    The \emph{(Weil divisor) class group}\index{class group} of \(X\) is
    \[\Cl(X) = \frac{\Div(X)}{\Prin(X)}\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    Some basic facts
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item If \(A\) is a Noetherian domain, then \(A\) is a UFD if and only if \(A\) is integrally closed, and \(\Cl(\Spec(A)) = 0\). Moreover, there exists \(A\) such that \(\Spec(A)\) has non-trivial class group.
        
        In particular, \(\Cl(\aff k n) = 0\).
        \item \(\Cl(\P^n_k) = \Z\),
        \item if \(Z \sse X\) is closed, with \(U = X \setminus Z\), then there exists a surjective map
        \begin{align*}
            \Cl(X) &\to \Cl(U) \\
            [Y] &\mapsto [Y \cap U]
        \end{align*}
        where on the right hand side, we set \([\emptyset] = 0\).
        \item if \(Z\) has codimension at least two, then the map \(\Cl(X) \to \Cl(U)\) is an isomorphism.
        \item if \(Z \sse X\) is integral, closed, codimension \(1\), then there exists an exact sequence

        \[\begin{tikzcd}
            \Z & {\Cl(X)} & {\Cl(U)} & 0
            \arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
            \arrow[from=1-2, to=1-3]
            \arrow[from=1-3, to=1-4]
        \end{tikzcd}\]
        where the map from \(\Z\) to \(\Cl(X)\) sends \(1\) to \([Z]\). 
    \end{enumerate}
    We call (iii), (iv), (v) \emph{excision}\index{excision}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of (ii)] Let \(D \sse \P^n\) be integral closed codimension \(1\). Then \(D = \van(f)\) where \(f\) is homogeneous, of degree \(d\). Define \(\deg(D) = d\). 

    Now extend linearly to get a homomorphism \(\deg : \Div(\P^n_k) \to \Z\). We claim that this is an isomorphism \(\Cl(D) \to \Z\). First, if \(f = g/h\) is a rational function on \(\P^n_k\), i.e. a ratio of homogeneous polynomials of the same degree, then
    \[\deg(\div(f)) = 0\]

    For surjectivity, take \(H = \van(X_0)\), where \(X_0\). For injectivity, say
    \[D = \sum_i n_{Y_i}[Y_i]\]
    If \(\sum n_{y_i}\deg(Y_i) = 0\), write \(Y_i = \van(g_i)\), where \(g_i\) is homoegrnous. Set
    \[f = \prod_i g_i^{n_{Y_i}}\]
    Then \(f\) is a homoegenous rational function of degree zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of excision] For (iii), \(k(X)\) and \(k(U)\) are naturally isomorphic, and so principal divisors are sent to principal divisors, and the map is well defined. For surjectivity, for \(D \sse U\) a prime Weil divisor, its closure \(\overline D\) in \(X\) is a prime Weil divisor on \(X\), with \(\overline D \cap U = D\).

    For (iv), \(Z\) does not even enter into the definitions. Equivalently, there isn't even a prime divisor contained in \(Z\).

    For (v), the kernel of the restriction \(\Cl(X) \to \Cl(U)\), is just divisors in \(X\) contained in \(Z\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cartier divisors}

We would like to study things locally looking like a principal ideals. Recall a height \(1\) prime in a UFD is principal.

\begin{definition}
    [Cartier divisor]
    A \emph{Cartier divisor}\index{Cartier divisor} is a section of the sheaf \(\mcK_X^* / \mcO_X^*\).
\end{definition}

Let \(X\) be a scheme, take the presheaf 
\[U = \Spec(A)\mapsto S^{-1}A\]
where \(S\) is the set of all non-zero divisors\footnote{non-(zero divisors).}. Sheafify this, and call the result \(\mcK_X\). This is a sheaf of rings, and take \(\mcK_X^* \sse \mcK_X\) for the subsheaf of invertible elements. This is a sheaf of abelian groups. Similarly, \(\mcO_X^*\) is the subsheaf of \(\mcO_X\), consisting of invertible elements.

Practically, every section of \(\mcK_X^*/\mcO_X^*\) can be described by data
\[\{(U_i, f_i)\}\]
where the \(U_i\) is a cover of \(X\), \(f_i\) is a section of \(\mcK_X^*(U_i)\), such that on \(U_i \cap U_j\), we have that
\[\frac{f_i}{f_j} \in \mcO_X^*(U_i \cap U_j)\]

\lec

If \(X\) is integral, then \(\mcK_X\) is a constant sheaf with \(\mcO_{X, \eta_X} = \Frac(A)\), where \(\Spec(A) \sse X\) is open.

We have a surjective sheaf homomorphism \(\mcK^* \surj \mcK^*/\mcO^*\), but a global section of \(\mcK^*/\mcO^*\) need not be the image of a global section of \(\mcK^*\).

\begin{definition}
    [principal Cartier divisors, Cartier class group]
    The image of \(\Gamma(X, \mcK^*_X)\) in \(\Gamma(X, \mcK^*_X/\mcO^*_X)\) is called the set of \emph{principal Cartier divisors}\index{principal Cartier divisor}. The quotient
    \[\frac{\Gamma(X, \mcK^*/\mcO^*)}{\im(\Gamma(X, K^*) \to \Gamma(X, \mcK^*/\mcO^*))}\]
    is called the \emph{Cartier class group of \(X\)}\index{Cartier class group}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Practically, a section \(\mcD \in \Gamma(X, \mcK^*/\mcK^*)\) can be specified by \(\{(U_i, f_i)\}\), where \(\{U_i\}\) forms an open cover, \(f_i \in \mcK^*_X(U_i)\) is a section, such that on \(U_i \cap U_j\),
    \[\frac{f_i}{f_j} \in \mcO_X^*(U_i \cap U_j)\]
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
    Let \(X\) be an integral, Noetherian, separated, regular in codimension \(1\) scheme. Given a Cartier divisor \(\mcD \in \Gamma(X, \mcK^*/\mcO^*)\), we get a Weil divisor by the rule: If \(Y \sse X\) is prime Weil, it has a generic point \(\eta_Y\). Now represent \(\mcD\) by \(\{U_i, f_i\}\), set
    \[n_Y = \nu_Y(f_i)\]
    for some \(U_i\) containing \(\eta_Y\). We then have a divisor
    \[\sum_{Y \sse X \text{ codimension }1\text{ integral}}n_Y[Y]\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    If \(\eta_Y\) is contained in \(U_i\) and \(U_j\), then the valuations of \(f_i\) and \(f_j\) differ by \(\nu_Y(f_i/F_j)\), but \(f_i/f_j\) is a unit, so it has valuation \(0\). Thus, this also tells us that it is independent of the choice of representative.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    If \(X\) is Noetherian, integral, separated, and all local rings \(\mcO_{X, x}\) are UFDs\footnote{This is called \emph{locally factorial}\index{locally factorial}. Note this implies that \(X\) is regular at codimension \(1\)}, then the association
    \[\left\{\text{Cartier divisors}\right\} \to \left\{\text{Weil divisors}\right\}\]
    constructed above is a bijection, and respects principal divisors. That is, it defines an isomorphism of class groups.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    [Sketch proof] All height \(1\) primes in a UFD are principal. For \(x \in X\), \(\mcO_{X, x}\) is a UFD, and so given a Weil divisor \(D\), we can restrict it to \(\Spec(\mcO_{X, x}) \to X\). That is, we take a fibre product. On \(\Spec(\mcO_{X, x})\), \(D\) is given by \(\van(f_x)\) as \(\mcO_{X, x}\) is a UFD, and prime Weil corresponds to height one primes, which are principal. Now \(f_x\) extends to a neighbourhood \(U_x\) of \(x\). Now glue these \(f_x\) to form a Cartier divisor.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    GIven a Cartier divisor \(\mcD\) on \(X\), with representatives \(\{U_i, f_i\}\), let
    \(L(\mcD) \sse \mcK_X\) be the sub \(\mcO_X\)-module generated on \(U_i\) by \(f_i^{-1}\),
\end{definition}

Keep in mind if \(A\) is an integral domain, \(X = \Spec(A)\), \(\mcD = \{(X, f)\}\) where \(f \in A\), then \(A_f \sse \Frac(A)\) is an \(A\)-module, generated by \(1/f\).

\begin{proposition}
    The sheaf \(L(\mcD)\) is a line bundle, i.e. it is locally free of rank \(1\)\footnote{locally (free of rank \(1\))}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    On \(U_i\), we have an isomorphism
    \begin{align*}
        \mcO(U_i) &\to L(\mcD)(U_i) \\
        1 &\mapsto \frac{1}{f_i}
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\underline{Important exercise:} If \(X = \P^n_k\), \(D\) to be the Weil divisor given by \(\van(x_0)\). Say \(\mcD\) is the correspoding Cartier divisor. Now show that
\[\mcO_{\P^n}(1) \cong L(\mcD)\]

\begin{remark}
    A line bundle \(L\) on \(X\) has a inverse under tensor product, namely
    \[L^{-1} = \Hom_{\mcO_X}(L, \mcO_X)\]
    Moreover, tensor product of line bundles are line bundles. If all Weil divisors are Cartier, then \(L(D + E) = L(D) \otimes L(E)\).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [Picard group] The \emph{Picard group}\index{Picard group} on \(X\), denoted by \(\Pic(X)\), is the group of line bundles on \(X\) up to isomorphism, with group operation being tensor product. 
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    Under mild assumptions, for example \(X \to \Spec(k)\) being projective, or \(X\) is integral, then the map
    \begin{align*}
        \text{Cartier divisors on }X &\to \Pic(X) \\
        \mcD &\mapsto L(\mcD)
    \end{align*}
    is surjective, and the kernel is exactly the principal Cartier divisors.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Omitted. See Abelian Varieties for more details.
\end{proof}
