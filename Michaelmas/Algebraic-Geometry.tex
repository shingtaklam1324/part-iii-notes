\subsection*{Why suffer through this? (Weil conjectures)}

The Weil conjectures link two worlds. The starting data is a homogeneous polynomial \(f \in \Z[x_0, \dots, x_n] = \Z[\bf x]\), of degree \(d\).

\textbf{World 1:} We can consider the vanishing locus

\[X = \bb V(f) = \left\{(a_0: \dots : a_n) \mid f(a_0, \dots, a_n) = 0\right\} \subseteq \bb P^1_\C\]

which is a projective hypersurface. We will assume that \(X\) is smooth, i.e. there is no point \(p \in X\), such that all of the partial derivatives \(\pdv{f}{x_i}(p)\) vanish for all \(i\). If we view \(\bb P^n\) as \((\C^{n+1} \setminus 0)/\text{scaling}\), and \(\C^{n+1}\setminus 0\) is given the Euclidean topology, then \(X\) is a (complex) manifold. This follows from the inverse/implicit function theorem. Moreover, \(X\) is a compact topological space. In fact, \(X\) can be triangulated. 

With this, we can consider various invariants associated to \(X\). The simplest is the Euler characteristic \(\chi(X)\). For example, \(\chi(\bb P^k) = k+1\). More generally, we can consider the Betti numbers \(b_i\), where

\[b_i = \dim_\Q H_i(X; \Q)\]

are the dimension of the rational homology groups. In this case,

\[\chi(X) = \sum_i(-1)^i b_i\]

\textbf{World 2:} Fix a prime \(p\), such that \(X\) is smooth over \(\overline{\bb F_p}\) (exercise: \(p\) exists). Define \(N_m\) to be the number of points in \(X\left(\bb F_{p^m}\right)\).

We can package the data of the \(N_m\)s: Consider the formal power series

\[\zeta(X, t) = \exp(\sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{N_m}{m} t^m)\]

which is the \emph{Weil \(\zeta\) function}.

\begin{theorem}
    [Grothendieck]

    \leavevmode

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(\zeta(X, t)\) is a rational functon. That is, \[\zeta(X, t) = \frac{P_0(t)\cdots P_{2n-2}(t)}{P_1(t)\cdots P_{2n-3}(t)}\] where each \(P_i(t)\) is a polynomial.
        \item The degree of the polynomial \(P_i(t)\) is \(b_i(X)\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Deligne proved the Riemann Hypothesis for the Weil \(\zeta\) function.

\section{Beyond Algebraic Varieties}

\subsection{Summary of affine varieties}

Let \(k = \overline k\) be an algebraically closed field. We would like to consider subsets

\[\van(S) \subseteq \aff k n\]

where \(S \subseteq k\poly n\). Up to isomorphism, this is the same as finitely generated \(k\)-algebras without nilpotent elements. Given \(V = \bb V(S)\), we have an ideal \[I(V) = \left\{f \in k\poly n \mid f = 0 \text{ on }V\right\}\]

We can then define

\[k[V] = \frac{k\poly n}{I(V)}\]

Conversely, given \(k[V]\), a finitely generated nilpotent-free \(k\)-algebra. Choosing generators \(y_1, \dots, y_n\) of the algebra, we have a surjection \(\pi: k[x_1, \dots, x_n] \surj k[V]\), sending \(x_i\) to \(y_i\). We can then take

\[V = \van(\ker(\pi))\]

\lec

Let \(\sqrt I\) be the radical of an ideal \(I\) be

\[\sqrt I = \left\{f \in k\polys \mid f^m \in I \text{ for some }m\right\}\]

Then \(\van(S) = \van(\inner{S}) = \van(\sqrt{\inner{S}})\), where \(\inner{S}\) is the ideal generated by \(S\). In this case, we have a correspondence between affine varieties in \(\aff k n\) and radical ideals in \(k\polys\).

\begin{remark}
    This awkwardness caused by ``in affine space'' can be removed, by considering varieties up to isomorphism, and coordinate rings (i.e. finitely generated nilpotent free \(k\)-algebras).
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Basic structures}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \(k\polys\) is the ring of algebraic functions on \(\aff k n\).
    \item \(I \subseteq k\polys\) is a radical ideal.
    \item The coordinate ring of \(V = \van(I)\) is \(k[V] = k\polys/I\).
\end{itemize}

This coordinate ring is the basic object, it's more fundamental than \(I\).

\subsubsection{Topology}

Fix an affine variety \(V \subseteq \aff k n\). It has a Zariski topology, where the closed sets are subsets of the form

\[V \cap \van(S)\]

where \(S \subseteq k\polys\). Equivalently, we can consider the Zariski topology on \(\aff k n\), and then equip \(V\) with the subspace topology.

Intrinsically, we can say that \(W \subseteq V\) is closed if we have that

\[W = \van(J)\]

where \(J \subseteq k[V]\) is an ideal. Exercise: these two topologies are the same.

\subsubsection{Nullstellensatz}

That is, what are the maximal ideals in \(k\polys\)? Equivalently, in a nilpotent free finitely generated \(k\)-algebra \(k[V]\)?

Fix \(V = \van(I)\), and pick \(p \in V\). There is a natural ring homomorphism \(\ev_p : k[V] \to k\), given by \(\ev_p(f) = f(p)\).

\begin{claim}
    \(\ev_p\) is surjective.
\end{claim}

\begin{proof}
    Exercise.
\end{proof}

The kernel of \(\ev_p\) is a maximal ideal in \(k[V]\). Therefore, points on \(V\) are one source of maximal ideals.

\begin{theorem}
    [Nullstellensatz] There is a bijection
    
    \begin{align*}
        \left\{\text{points in }V\right\} &\bij \left\{\text{maximal ideals in }k[V]\right\}
    \end{align*}
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection{Functions}

Each \(f \in k[V]\) gives a function \(V \to k (= \aff k 1)\), sending \(p\) to \(f(p)\). Equivalently, if we denote \(\mfm_p = \ker(\ev_p)\), then this is the quotient map

\[k[V] \surj k = k[V]/\mfm_p\]

\subsubsection{Morphisms}

Given \(V \subseteq \aff k n\), \(W \subseteq \aff k m\), morphisms \(V \to W\) are constructed as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Take functions \(f_1, \dots, f_m \in k\poly n\).
    \item This gives a map \(\varphi = (f_1, \dots, f_m) : V \to \aff k m\).
    \item A morphism \(V \to W\) must additionally satisfy that \(\varphi(V) \subseteq W\).
\end{enumerate}

There is a more intrinsic construction. Any such \(\varphi : V \to W\) as above, gives rise to a pullback map \(\varphi^* : k[W] \to k[V]\), where \(\varphi^*(g) = g \circ \varphi\).

There is a bijection

\[\set{\text{Morphisms }V \to W} \bij \set{k\text{-algebra homomorphisms }k[W] \to k[V]}\]

In practice, the right hand side is much cleaner than the right hand side.

\underline{Lesson:} We \(\heartsuit\) coordinate rings.

\underline{Upshot:} Let \(R\) be a finitely generated, nilpotent free \(k\)-algebra, the topological space

\[X_R = \left\{\mfm \subseteq R \mid \mfm \text{ is a maximal ideal}\right\}\]

Closed sets are given by \(\van(S)\), for \(S \subseteq R\).

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{question}
    What is an abstract (not necessarily affine) variety?
\end{question}

Morally, it's an object which locally looks like an affine variety.

\begin{example}
    [Non-algebraically closed fields] Consider the ideal

    \[I = \inner{x^2 + y^2 + 1} \in \R[x, y]\]

    Then \(\van(I) = \emptyset \subseteq \R^2\). \(I\) is a prime ideal, therefore radical, so the na\"ive Nullstellensatz fails.
\end{example}

\begin{question}
    Given a commutative ring \(R\), is there a natural topological space \(X_R\), on which \(R\) is the ring of functions? For example, \(R = \Z, \Z[x]\) and so on.
\end{question}

\begin{example}
    [Why restrict to radical ideals] Assume \(k = \overline k\).

    \[C = \van(y - x^2) \subseteq \aff k n\]

    and

    \[D = \van(y)\]

    Then

    \[C \cap D = \van(y, y-x^2) = \van(x^2, y) = \left\{(0, 0)\right\}\]

    But \(\inner{y, y-x^2}\) has radical \(\inner{x, y}\), it is not equal to the ideal \(\inner{x, y}\). The intersection wants to be two points. Take

    \[D_{\delta} = \van(y + \delta)\]

    where \(\delta \in k \setminus 0\), then \(C \cap D_\delta\) has two points.
\end{example}

\subsection{Spectrum of a ring}

\underline{Basic idea:} Given a ring \(A\), we will define

\[\Spec(A) = \left\{\mfp \subseteq A \mid \mfp \text{ a prime ideal}\right\}\]

Why prime?

\begin{itemize}
    \item Functoriality - \(A \to B\) induces \(\Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\) by pullback.
    \item Some rings have very few maximal ideals.
\end{itemize}

\lec

Let \(A\) be a commutative ring,

\underline{Goal:} Define a topological space \(X_A\) on which \(A\) is naturally the ring of functions.

\begin{definition}
    [Zariski spectrum] The Zariski spectrum of \(A\) is

    \[\Spec(A) = \set{\mfp \subseteq A \mid \mfp \text{ a prime ideal}}\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    We might also consider the maximal ideal spectrum, but this fails to be functorial. That is, a ring homomorphism \(\varphi: A \to B\) induces a map \(\varphi^* = \varphi^{-1} : \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)\), by considering the preimage. On the other hand, no such map exists for maximal ideals.
\end{remark}

\underline{Why?} Whatever \(X_A\) could be, given a point \(p \in X_A\), we should have an evaluation map

\begin{align*}
    \ev_p : A &\to K_p \\
    f &\mapsto f(p)
\end{align*}

where \(K_p\) is a field. Note we allow the field \(K_p\) to depend on \(p\). Nor are we requiring that \(\ev_p\) be surjective. The image of \(\ev_p\) is an integral domain, and moreover, any map to an integral domain \(R\) is also a map to a field \(F = \Frac(R)\).

Subject to this, homomorphisms \(f : A \to K\), with \(K\) a field, corresponds to \(\ker(f)\), which is a prime ideal.

\begin{example}
    Taking \(A = \Z\), we have that

    \[\Spec(\Z) = \left\{\inner{p} \mid p \in \N \text{ a prime}\right\} \cup \left\{0\right\}\]

    Pick a function \(123 \in \Z\). Now evaluate at different points, which is just reduction modulo \(p\), i.e.

    \begin{align*}
        123(2) &= 1 \in \Z/2 \\
        123(3) &= 0 \in \Z/3 \\
        123(0) &= 123 \in \Z \subseteq \Q
    \end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    Taking \(A = \R[x]\), \(\Spec(A)\) has elements corresponding to 
    
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item irreducible quadratics in \(x\), e.g. \(\inner{x^2 + 1}\), which corresponds to \((\C\backslash\R)/\text{complex conjugation}\).
        \item linear polynomials in \(x\), e.g. \(\inner{x-2}\), corresponding to \(\R\),
        \item \(0\).
    \end{enumerate}

    Hence \(\Spec(A)\) is the upper half plane, plus the point zero.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    If \(A\) is an integral domain, then \(0 \in \Spec(A)\) is called the generic point.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    The value of \(f \in A\) at \(\mfp \in \Spec(A)\) is \(f + \mfp \in A/\mfp\).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Topology on \(\Spec(A)\)}

\underline{Motivation:} In the Zariski topology, the zero sets should be the vanishing sets of functions. Fix \(f \in A\), define the vanishing set of \(f\) to be

\[\van(f) = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid f \in \mfp\right\} = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid f(\mfp) = 0 \in A/\mfp\right\}\]

More generally, if \(I \subseteq A\) is an ideal, then

\[\van(I) = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid I \subseteq \mfp\right\} = \left\{\mfp \in \Spec(A) \mid f(\mfp) = 0 \text{ for all }f\in I\right\}\]

\begin{definition}
    The sets \(\van(I) \subseteq \Spec(A)\), for \(I \subseteq A\) ideals, satisfy the requirements for closed sets in a topological space. 
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}
    Easy to see \(\emptyset\) and \(\Spec(A)\) are closed. Moreover,

    \[\bigcap_\alpha \van(I_\alpha) = \van\left(\sum_\alpha I_\alpha\right)\]

    and

    \[\van(I_1) \cup \van(I_2) = \van(I_1 \cap I_2)\]

    For this, \(\subseteq\) is clear. For the reverse containment,

    \[I_1I_2 \subseteq I_1\cap I_2\]

    If \(\mfp \supseteq I_1 \cap I_2\), then it contains \(I_1I_2\). Since \(\mfp\) is prime, then either \(I_1\) or \(I_2\) is contained in \(\mfp\).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
    Let \(k = \overline k\), and \(A = k[x, y]\). Then
    
    \[\Spec(A) \supseteq \maxSpec(A) = k^2\]

    where \(\maxSpec(A)\) is the set of maximal ideals, which by the Nullstellensatz corresponds to the points in \(\aff k 2\). There is a point for each non-maximal prime ideal, e.g.

    \[\inner{x - y} \quad \inner{y^2 - x^3}\quad\inner{y-x^2}\]

    Consider the generic point \(0 \in \Spec(A)\). Then the closure of \(\{0\}\) is \(\Spec(A)\). On the other hand, the closure of a maximal ideal is itself.

    Now consider the ideal \(\inner{f}\), where \(f\) is an irreducible polynomial, e.g. \(y^2 - x^3\). The closure of \(\{\inner{f}\}\) contains every maximal ideal \(\inner{x - a, y- b}\) such that \(b^2 - a^3 = 0\), since such maximal ideals contain \(\inner{f}\).
\end{example}

Consider the ring \(A = \C\), the ring of formal power series in \(x\). Then

\[\Spec(A) = \left\{\inner{0}, \inner{x}\right\}\]

which

\begin{align*}
    \overline{\set{\inner{0}}} &= \Spec(A) \\
    \overline{\set{\inner{x}}} &= \set{\inner{x}}
\end{align*}

On the other hand, \(\maxSpec(A)\) is a single (closed) point, which is the same as \(\Spec(\C)\).

\lec

\(\Spec(A)\) is a local model of a \underline{scheme}. Last time, we considered \(\Spec(A)\) as a set, and as a topological space.

\subsection{Functions on open sets in \(\Spec(A)\)}

\begin{definition}
    Let \(f \in A\). The distinguished open corresponding to \(f\) is

    \[U_f = F(f) = \Spec(A) \setminus \van(f)\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    The distinguised open sets form a base for the Zariski topology on \(\Spec(A)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Examples sheet 1.
\end{proof}

\underline{Starting point:} We are already thinking about \(A\) as ``functions on \(\Spec(A)\)''.

\begin{lemma}
    The subspace \(U_f \subseteq \Spec(A)\) is (naturally) homeomorphic to \(\Spec(A_f)\), where \(A_f\) is the localisation of \(A\) at \(f\).
\end{lemma}

\underline{Aside on localisation:} \(A_f = A[t]/\inner{tf - 1}\). For example, if \(A = \C[x]\) and \(f = x\), then \(A_f = \C[x, x^{-1}]\). There is an obvious map \(A \to A_f\), however if \(f\) is a zero divisor, this map may not be injective.

Since we have a ring homomorphism \(A \to A_f\), we have an induced map \(\Spec(A_f) \to \Spec(A)\).

\begin{proof}
    Primes in \(A_f\) are exactly in bijection with primes in \(A\) that do not contain \(f\). Why? Let \(\theta : A \to A_f\) be the natural map.

    \begin{claim}
        Every ideal in \(A_f\) is generated by \(\theta(I)\) for some ideal \(I \subseteq A\).
    \end{claim}

    For details, see Atiyah-MacDonald, or Commutative Algebra.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
    Let \(A = \C[x, y]\), \(f = xy\). Then 

    \[U_f = \C^2 \setminus \left(\set{x\text{-axis}} \cup \set{y\text{-axis}}\right) \cup \set{\text{weird, i.e. non-closed, points}}\]

    A more algebraic way of thinking about this is that

    \[A_f = \frac{\C[x, y][t]}{\inner{txy - 1}} \cong \C[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]\]

    If a prime ideal \(\mfp\) in \(\C[x, y]\) contains \(x\) or \(y\), then the ideal generated by the image of \(\mfp\) is the unit ideal. 
\end{example}

This sets up the following association:

\begin{align*}
    \text{Distinguished open set} &\to \text{Rings} \\
    U_f &\mapsto A_f
\end{align*}

\underline{Observe:} If \(U_{f_1} \subseteq U_{f_2}\), there is a natural ring homomorphism \(A_{f_2} \to A_{f_1}\).

\section{Sheaves}

Sheaves will formalise behaviour of the assignment above.

\subsection{Presheaves}

\begin{definition}
    [presheaf]
    A \emph{presheaf \(\mcF\) of abelian groups} on a topological space \(X\) is an assignment

    \begin{align*}
        \text{Open sets in }X &\to \text{Abelian groups} \\
        U &\mapsto \mcF(U)
    \end{align*}

    such that if \(U \subseteq V\), then there is a homomorphism \(\res^V_U : \mcF(V) \to \mcF(U)\), with

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\res_U^U = \id_{\mcF(U)}\),
        \item if we have \(U \subseteq V \subseteq W\) opens, then
        \[\res_U^V \circ \res_V^W = \res_U^W\]
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\underline{Keep in mind:} If \(X\) is any topological space, then

\[U \mapsto \left\{f : U \to \R \mid f \text{ is continuous}\right\}\]

is a presheaf.

\begin{remark}
    \(\mcF\) is just a functor between certain categories. It is easy to define presheaves of sets, rings, etc.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{Morphisms of presheaves}

Let \(\mcF, \mcG\) be presheaves on a topologial space \(X\).

\begin{definition}
    A morphism \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) is, for each open set \(U\), a homomorphism

    \[\varphi_U : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\]

    compatible with restriction. That is, if \(V \subseteq U\) are open, then

\subsection{Sheaves - Definitions and Examples}

\begin{definition}
    [Sheaf] A sheaf \(\mcF\) on \(X\) is a presheaf such that

    \begin{enumerate}[label=S\arabic*.]
        \item If \(U \subseteq X\) is open, and \(\set{U_i}\) is an open cover of \(U\), and if \(s \in \mcF(U)\) is such that
        \[s\vert_{U_i} = \res_{U_i}^U(s) = 0\]
        for all \(i\), then \(s = 0\).
        \item If \(U\) and \(\set{U_i}\) are as above, given \(s_i \in \mcF(U_i)\), with \(s_i\vert_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j\vert_{U_i \cap U_j}\) for all \(i, j\), then there exists \(s \in \mcF(U)\) with \(s\vert_{U_i} = s_i\) for all \(i\).  
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    A morphism of sheaves is a morphism of the underlying presheaves.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    [Non-example]

    Let \(X = \C\) with the Euclidean topology. Set

    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to \C \mid f\text{ bounded analytic}\right\}\]

    This is \emph{not} a sheaf, since it does not satisfy S2.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [Non-example]

    Fix an abelian group \(G\), and set \(\mcF(U) = G\) for all \(U\). This is a presheaf, but not a sheaf. If \(U_1, U_2\) are disjoint, then the sheaf axioms force \(\mcF(U_1 \cup U_2) = G \times G\).
\end{example}

\lec

\begin{example}
    [Constant sheaf] Fix an abelian group \(G\), define the constant sheaf with values in \(G\) to be 

    \[\mcF(U) = \left\{f : U \to G \mid f \text{ locally constant on }U\right\}\]

    In practice, if \(U\) is connected, then \(\mcF(U) = G\). Otherwise, \(\mcF(U)\) is \(G^{\#\text{connected components}}\). In particular, if we take \(G = \Z\), then this sheaf is denoted \(\underline{\Z}\), and

    \[\underline{Z}(X) = \Z^{\#\text{connected components}} = \HH_0(X, \Z)\]

    This leads to the question, what about the other (co)homology groups?
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    [Structure sheaf]
    Let \(V\) be an irreducible affine variety on \(k = \overline k\). Define a sheaf \(\mcO_V\) as follows,

    \[\mcO_V(U) = \left\{f : k(V) \mid f \text{ is regular at all }p \in U\right\}\]

    This is called the structure sheaf. Note \(k(V) = \Frac(k[V])\).
\end{example}

\subsection{Basic constructions}

Fix a sheaf \(\mcF\) on a topological space \(X\).

\underline{Terminology:} A section of \(\mcF\) over \(U \subseteq X\) is an element of \(\mcF(U)\). The group of global sections is \(\mcF(X)\).

\begin{definition}
    [Stalk] Fix a point \(p \in X\). Then the stalk of \(\mcF\) at \(p\), denoted \(\mcF_p\), is

    \[\mcF_p = \frac{\left\{(s, U) \mid s \in \mcF(U), p \in U\right\}}{\sim}\]

    where \((s, u)\sim (s', u')\) if there exists a nonempty open \(p \in W \subseteq U \cap U'\), such that \(s\vert_W = s'\vert_W\).
\end{definition}

\(\mcF_p\) is sections of \(\mcF\) on some neighbourhood of \(p\).

\subsubsection*{Group structure on \(\mcF_p\)}

Given \((s, U), (t, V)\) representing elements of \(\mcF_p\), we can define

\[(s, U) + (t, V) = (s\vert_{W} + t\vert_W, W)\]

for any neighbourhood \(W \subseteq U \cap V\) of \(p\).

\begin{example}
    Consider the structure sheaf \(\mcO_{\aff k 1}\), where \(k = \overline k\). Open sets of \(\aff k 1\) are complements of finite sets (and the empty set).

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\mcO_{\aff k 1} = k[x]\).
        \item \(U = \aff k 1 \setminus \set 1\), then \(\mcO_{\aff k 1}(U) = k[x, (x-1)^{-1}]\) (c.f. localisation).
    \end{itemize}

    Therefore, \(\mcO_{\aff k 1, 0} = \left\{f(x)/g(x) \mid g(x) \ne 0\right\}\), is the local ring of \(\aff k 1\) at \(0\).
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
    Let \(\mcF, \mcG\) be sheaves on \(X\). Let \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\) be a morphism. If the induced maps \(f_p : \mcF_p \to \mcG_p\) is an isomorphism for all \(p \in X\), then \(\mcF\) is an isomorphism.
\end{proposition}

\underline{Exercise:} The induced map makes sense.

\begin{proof}
    We will show that for every open \(U \subseteq X\), the morphism \(f_U : \mcF(U) \to \mcG(U)\) is an isomorphism. Given this, we will define the inverse map \(f^{-1} : \mcG \to \mcF\) to be \(f_U^{-1}\) on each \(U\).

    \underline{Exercise:} This is compatible with gluing.

    \textbf{Injectivity.} Suppose \(s \in \mcF(U)\), with \(f_U(s) = 0\). This determines elements \(s_p \in \mcF_p\) for all \(p \in U\). These are all zero, since \(f_p(s_p) = 0 \in \mcG_p\), and \(f_p\) is injective. There exists open sets \(U_p\) around each \(p \in U\), such that \(s\vert_{U_p} = 0\), by S2. But then by S1., \(s = 0\).

    \textbf{Surjectivity.} Let \(t \in \mcG(U)\). We will build \(s \in \mcF(U)\) with \(f_U(s) = t\). Let \(t_p \in \mcG_p\) for the image of \(t\). Denote \(s_p = f_p^{-1}(t_p)\). This makes sense since \(f_p\) is an isomorphism. Note that \emph{a priori}, we don't know that the \(s_p\) give us an element \(s \in \mcF(U)\).

    Find a representative \((s_{V(p)}, V(p))\) of \(s_p\), with \(V(p)\) a neighbourhood of \(p\), such that 
    
    \[f_{V(p)}((s_{V(p)}, V(p))) \sim (t, U)\]

    Why do the \(s_{V(p)}\) glue over different choices of \(p\)? They glue because we have already shown injectivity of \(f_U\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item Even easier than the proposition, there is a natural map
        \[\mcF(U) \to \prod_{p \in U}\mcF_p\]
        and the map is injective.
        \item Given \(\varphi, \psi : \mcF \to \mcG\), with \(\varphi_p = \psi_p\) for all \(p \in X\). Then \(\varphi = \psi\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{Sheafification}

``The best way of turning a presheaf into a sheaf''

\begin{definition}
    [sheafification] Let \(\mcF\) be a presheaf on \(X\). A morphism \(\sh : \mcF \to \mcF^{\sh}\) is a sheafification if \(\mcF^{\sh}\) is a sheaf, and for any morphism \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\), with \(\mcG\) a sheaf, there exists a unique commuting diagram

    % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsMyxbMCwwLCJcXG1jRiJdLFsyLDAsIlxcbWNGXlxcc2giXSxbMSwyLCJcXG1jRyJdLFswLDIsIlxcdmFycGhpIiwyXSxbMCwxLCJcXHNoIl0sWzEsMiwiXFxleGlzdHMhIl1d
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	\mcF && {\mcF^{\sh}} \\
	\\
	& \mcG
	\arrow["\varphi"', from=1-1, to=3-2]
	\arrow["\sh", from=1-1, to=1-3]
	\arrow["{\exists!}", from=1-3, to=3-2]
\end{tikzcd}\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item If \(\sh : \mcF \to \mcF^{\sh}\) exists, then it is unique up to unique isomorphism.
        \item Functoriality. Presheaf morphisms induce morphisms of sheaves. That is, we have that
        % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNCxbMCwwLCJcXG1jRiJdLFsyLDAsIlxcbWNHIl0sWzAsMiwiXFxtY0Zee1xcc2h9Il0sWzIsMiwiXFxtY0dee1xcc2h9Il0sWzAsMl0sWzAsMV0sWzEsM10sWzIsM11d
        \[\begin{tikzcd}
            \mcF && \mcG \\
            \\
            {\mcF^{\sh}} && {\mcG^{\sh}}
            \arrow[from=1-1, to=3-1]
            \arrow[from=1-1, to=1-3]
            \arrow[from=1-3, to=3-3]
            \arrow[from=3-1, to=3-3]
        \end{tikzcd}\]
        commutes.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

Note that if \(\mcF\) is a presheaf on \(X\), then it has stalks \(\mcF_p\) for each \(p \in X\), and an element of \(\mcF_p\) is called a germ of \(\mcF\) at \(p\).

\begin{proposition}
    Let \(\mcF\) be a presheaf on \(X\). Define

    \begin{align*}
        \mcF^{\sh}(U) = \big\{(f_p)_{p \in U} \mid f_p \in \mcF_p &\text{ and for every }p\text{, there exists an open }V_p \subseteq U\text{ neighbourhood of }p, \\ &\text{ and a section }s \in \mcF(V_p)\text{ such that }s_q = f_q \in \mcF_q\text{ for all }q \in V_p\big\}
    \end{align*}
\end{proposition}

We are doing two things here. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We are adding sections, coming from coherent germs.
    \item Throwing away sections that are incoherent.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{proof}
    The restriction maps are clear. The map \(\mcF \to \mcF^{\sh}\) is the obvious one, namely \(s \mapsto (s_p)_{p \in U}\).

    \underline{Exercise:} Verify the universal property. A hint for the exercise: if we have a sheaf \(\mcG\) and a morphism \(f : \mcF \to \mcG\). On an open set \(U\), if we have a section \(s \in \mcF(U)\), that restricts to zero on an open cover, then its image in \(\mcG(U)\) is zero, as \(\mcG\) is a sheaf.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    [of the construction] If \(\mcF\) is a presheaf on \(X\), then \(\mcF\) and \(\mcF^{\sh}\) have the same stalks at every \(p \in X\).
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Kernels, cokernels, etc.}

Let \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) be a morphism of presheaves on \(X\). A presheaf kernel/image/cokernel assigns \(U\) to 

\[\ker(\varphi_U), \im(\varphi_U), \coker(\varphi_U)\] 

respectively.

\underline{Exercise:} Suppose \(\varphi : \mcF \to \mcG\) is a morphism of sheaves. Then the presheaf kernel of \(\varphi\) is a sheaf.

On the other hand, the image and cokernels need not be sheaves.

\begin{example}
    Let \(X = \C\) with the usual Euclidean topology. Consider the sheaf \(\mcO_X\) of holomorphic functions under addition. Another sheaf is \(\mcO_X^*\), whose abelian groups are

    \[\mcO_X^*(U) = \left\{f \text{ holomorphic on }U \text{ and nonwhere zero}\right\}\]

    under multiplication. 
    
    \underline{Key point:} Exponentiation makes things nonzero, and logarithms exists only locally.

    Define the exponentiation map

    \begin{align*}
        \exp : \mcO_X(U) \to \mcO_X^*(U) \\
        \exp(f) = e^f
    \end{align*}

    Do all sections of \(\mcO_X^*\) arise in this way? Namely by exponentiation. Take

    \begin{align*}
        U_1 &= \C \setminus \Ico{0, \infty}\\
        U_2 &= \C \setminus \Ioc{-\infty, 0}
    \end{align*}

    Take \(U = U_1 \cup U_2 = \C\setminus \set{0}\). Take a function \(z \in \mcO_X^*(U)\). This lies in the presheaf cokernel of \(\exp\), because the logarithm does not exists on \(U\). However, on \(U_1, U_2\), \(\log\) exists. So the cokernel is trivial.

    In conclusion, the image of \(\exp\) as a presheaf morphism is not a sheaf.
\end{example}

We'll see next time since the sheafification of

\[\coker(\exp : \mcO_X \to \mcO_X^*)\]

is \(0\), the exponential ``should be'' a surjective sheaf morphism.
