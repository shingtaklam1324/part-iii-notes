\section{Introduction}

A \emph{Lie group} is fundamentally a group, which also a (smooth) manifold. For example, \(\GL_n, \SL_n, \SO_n, \Sp_{2n}\).

\begin{example}
    A prototypical example of a Lie group is the circle group \(S^1\).
\end{example}

Let \(G\) be a Lie group. Then the \emph{Lie algebra}\index{Lie algebra!as tangent space} of \(G\) is the tangent space at the identity \(e\) of \(G\). That is, 
\[\mfg = \T_eG\]
\(\mfg\) is a vector space, with additional structure, which we will see later.

By taking a derivative, we turn the conjugation map
\begin{align*}
    G &\to \Aut(G) \\
    g &\mapsto g(\cdot)g^{-1}
\end{align*}
into a map
\begin{align*}
    \ad : \mfg \to \End(\mfg)
\end{align*}
called the \emph{adjoint}\index{adjoint}. This gives a bilinear map
\begin{align*}
    [\cdot, \cdot] : \mfg \times \mfg &\to \mfg \\
    [x, y] &= \ad(x)(y)
\end{align*}

\begin{example}
    If \(G = \GL_n(\R)\), then we have that \(\mfg = \gl_n(\R) = \Mat_n(\R)\), with
    \[[x, y] = xy - yx\]
\end{example}
What do Lie algebras tell us about the structure of the Lie group \(G\)? 
\begin{itemize}
    \item We will define the \emph{root system} of \(\mfg\), and this then tells us about commutator relations in \(G\) (see Carter's book).
    \item We will define the \emph{Weyl group} of \(\mfg\). For example, the Weyl group of \(\gl_n(\C)\) is isomorphic to \(S_n\), there is an embedding \(S_n \inj \GL_n(\C)\), vie permutation matrices. Let \(B\) denote the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices in \(\GL_n(\C)\), then there exists a \emph{Bruhat decomposition}
    \[G = \bigsqcup_{w \in S_n}BwB\]
\end{itemize}
Lie algebras also give us information of the representation theory of \(G\) and \(\mfg\). For example, there exists a bijection
\[\{\text{finite dimensional }\C\text{-representations of }\SL_n(\C)\} \bij \{\text{finite dimensional }\C\text{-representations of }\sl_n(\C)\}\]

Moreover, we can describe the right hand side completely.

In addition, Lie algebras have applications in Algebraic Geometry, for example, we can use Lie algebras to build families of surfaces, of equivalenrly, algebraic curves (See book by Slodovy).

We will define the \emph{Dynkin diagrams} of \emph{semisimple Lie algebras}. For example,

\section{Basic definitions and examples}

Let \(k\) be a field. Most of the time, \(k = \C\), but not always. We will sometimes point out how things can go wrong in characteristic \(p\).

\begin{definition}
    [Lie algebra]
    A \emph{Lie algebra}\index{Lie algebra} over \(k\) is a vector space \(\mfg\) over \(k\), together with a bilinear pairing
    \begin{align*}
        [\cdot, \cdot] : \mfg \times \mfg \to \mfg
    \end{align*}
    satisfying
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \([xx] = 0\) for all \(x \in \mfg\),
        \item the Jacobi identity
        \[[x[yz]] + [y[zx]] + [z[xy]] = 0\]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{notation}
    Note that when clear, we will write \([xy] := [x, y]\).
\end{notation}

\begin{remark}
    In particular, we have antisymmetry, i.e.
    \[[xy] = -[yx]\]
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [(Lie) subalgebra]
    A \(k\)-vector subspace \(\mfh\) of \(\mfg\) is a \emph{(Lie) subalgebra}\index{subalgebra@(Lie) subalgebra} if \(\mfh\) is closed under the Lie bracket of \(\mfg\). That is, for all \(x, y \in \mfh\), \([xy] \in \mfh\).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Let \(V\) be a finite dimensional \(k\)-vector space, then
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Let \(\gl(V) = \End(V)\), with \([xy] = xy-yx\). If we choose a basis for \(V\), then we can identify \(\gl(V)\) with \(\Mat_n(k)\). In this case, we will write \(\gl_n\) or \(\gl_n(k)\)\index{\(\gl_n\)}.
        \item Let
        \[\sl(V) = \left\{x \in \gl(V) \mid \tr(x) = 0\right\}\]
        This defines a subalgebra of \(\gl(V)\), called the \emph{special linear Lie algebra}, with \(\dim(\sl(V)) = \dim(V)^2 - 1\). The standard basis of \(\sl(V)\) is given by
        \begin{align*}
            E_{i,j} \text{ for }i \ne j \\
            E_{i, i} - E_{i+1, i+1}
        \end{align*}
        We will often write \(\sl_n\)\index{\(\sl_n\)} for this Lie algebra.       
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

        \item Suppose \(\Char(k) \ne 2\), and suppose \(V\) is endowed with a symmetric (non-degenerate?) bilinear form

        If \(M \in \GL(V)\) is such that \(\inner{v, w} = v^\T M w\), so

        We usually take
        \[M = \begin{cases}
            \begin{pmatrix}
                0 & I_\ell \\

                1 & 0 & 0 \\

                0 & I_\ell & 0
            \end{pmatrix} & n = 2\ell + 1
        \end{cases}\]
        These are called the \emph{orthogonal Lie algebra}\index{\(\so_n\)}, denoted \(\so_n\).
        \begin{remark}
            \label{rem:sl2c}
            In the case \(n = 2\), let
            \[e = \begin{pmatrix}
                0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0
            \end{pmatrix} \quad f = \begin{pmatrix}
                0 & 0 \\

            \end{pmatrix} \quad h = \begin{pmatrix}
                1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1
            \end{pmatrix}\]
            viewed as matrices (in \(\sl_2(\C)\)). Indeed, this is the standard basis of \(\sl_2(\C)\). Note
            \begin{align*}
                [ef]&= h \\
                [he] &= 2e \\
                [hf] &= -2f
            \end{align*}
            We'll see that (in some sense) the structure of all semisimple Lie algebras comes from \(\sl_2(\C)\).
        \end{remark}
        \item Again suppose \(\Char(k) \ne 2\). Now suppose \(V\) is endowed with a non-degenerate skew-symmetric (or alternating) bilinear form \(\inner{\cdot, \cdot}\), then
        \[\sp(V) = \left\{x \in \gl(V) \mid \inner{xv, w} + \inner{v, xw} = 0\right\}\]
        In coordinates, we take the form \(\inner{\cdot, \cdot}\) to be the skew-symmetric form given by
        \[M = \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & I_\ell \\
            -I_\ell & 0
        \end{pmatrix}\]
        where \(n = 2\ell\). This is called the \emph{symplectic Lie algebra}\index{\(\sp_n\)}, denoted \(\sp_n\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

        \setcounter{enumi}{4}
        \item Any vector space \(V\) is a Lie algebra with \([vw] = 0\) for all \(v, w\). We call such Lie algebras \emph{abelian}\index{abelian Lie algebra}. It is named like this, since for \emph{linear Lie algebras}\index{linear Lie algebra}, that is, any subalgebra of \(\gl(V)\) where \(V\) is finite dimensional, \([xy] = xy - yx = 0\) is true if and only if \(x\) and \(y\) commute.
        \item \(\mfb_n\) is the \emph{Borel algebra}\index{\(\mfb_n\)} of upper triangular matrices
        \[\begin{pmatrix}
            * & \cdots & * \\
            & \ddots & \vdots \\
            0& & *
        \end{pmatrix}\]
        This is the Lie algebra associated to the Borel subgroup of upper triangular invertible matrices.
        \item \(\mfn_n\)\index{\(\mfn_n\)} is the Lie algebra of strictly upper triangular matrices,
        \[\begin{pmatrix}
            0 & \cdots & * \\
            & \ddots & \vdots \\
            0& & 0
        \end{pmatrix}\]
        \(\mfn\) stands for \emph{nilpotent}, see section 7.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\section{Basic representation theory}

\begin{definition}
    [homomorphism, isomorphism]
    A linear map \(\varphi : \mfg \to \mfh\) between two Lie algebras is a \emph{homomorphism}\index{homomorphism} if
    \[\varphi([xy]) = [\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]\]
    for all \(x, y \in \mfg\). If \(\varphi\) is a linear isomorphism, we call \(\varphi\) an \emph{isomorphism of Lie algebras}\index{isomorphism!of Lie algebras}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [representation]
    A \emph{representation}\index{representation} of \(\mfg\) is a Lie algebra homomorphism
    \[\varphi : \mfg \to \gl(V)\]
    for some vector space \(V\).
\end{definition}

\begin{notation}
    We also call \(V\) itself a \emph{representation}, or a \(\mfg\)-module. We write \(\mfg \actson V\), and say \(\mfg\) acts on \(V\). We will write
    \[x \vdot v = xv := \varphi(x)(v)\]

    The \emph{dimension}\index{dimension} of the representation is the dimension of \(V\).
\end{notation}

\begin{example}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Let \(\dim(V) = 1\), then \(\mfg \actson V\) via \(xv = 0\). This is called the \emph{trivial representation}\index{trivial representation}.
        \item \(\mfg\) is a subalgebra of \(\gl(V)\), then the natural inclusion \(\mfg \inj \gl(V)\) is called the \emph{defining representation}\index{defining representation}.
        \item Let \(x \in \mfg\), define \(\ad_x : \mfg \to \mfg\) by \(\ad_x(y) = [xy]\). The map
        \begin{align*}
            \ad : \mfg &\to \gl(\mfg) \\
            x & \mapsto \ad_x
        \end{align*}
        is called the \emph{adjoint representation}\index{adjoint representation}.
        \begin{remark}
            Recall \(e, h, f\) from \cref{rem:sl2c}, the adjoint representation of \(\sl_2(\C)\) has matrices
            \[\ad_h = \begin{pmatrix}
                2 \\
                & 0 \\
                & & -2
            \end{pmatrix} \quad \ad_e = \begin{pmatrix}
                & -2 \\
                & & 1 \\
                &
            \end{pmatrix} \quad \ad_f = \begin{pmatrix}
                & \\
                -1 \\
                & 2 & \phantom{0}
            \end{pmatrix}\]
            with respect to the basis \(\{e,h,f\}\).
        \end{remark}
        \item If \(V, W\) are representations of \(\mfg\), then so is their \emph{direct sum}\index{direct sum} \(V \oplus V\), via
        \[x(v, w) = (xv, xw)\]
        \item If \(V\) is a representation of \(\mfg\), then so is the \emph{dual}\index{dual representation} \(V^*\),
        \[(xf)(v) = -f(xv)\]
        for all \(x \in \mfg, f \in V^*, v \in V\).
        \item If \(V, W\) are representations of \(\mfg\), then so is the \emph{homomorphisms}\index{hom representation@\(\Hom(V, W)\) representation} \(\Hom(V, W)\), via
        \[(x\vdot f)(v) = x\vdot f(v) - f(x \vdot v)\]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    [equivariant, isomorphism]
    If \(V, W\) are representations of \(\mfg\), then a linear map \(\varphi : V \to W\) is called \emph{\(\mfg\)-equivariant}\index{equivariant} for all \(x \in \mfg, v \in V\),
    \[x \vdot \varphi(v) = \varphi(x \vdot v)\]

    We say \(V, W\) are \emph{isomorphic}\index{isomorphism!of representations} if there exists a \(\mfg\) equivariant isomorphism (of vector spaces) \(V \to W\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [subrepresentation, irreducible] A \emph{subrepresentation}\index{subrepresentation} \(W \le V\) is a subspace \(xw \in W\) for all \(x \in \mfg, w \in W\). A non-zero representation \(V\) is \emph{irreducible}\index{irreducible representation} or \emph{simple}\index{simple representation} if the only subrepresentations of \(V\) are \(0\) and \(V\).
\end{definition}

\underline{Exercise:} The trivial representation is irreducible, and so are the defining and the adjoint representation of \(\sl_2(\C)\).

\lec

\begin{definition}
    [completely reducible, semisimple]
    A representation \(V\) is \emph{completely reducible}\index{completely reducible}, or \emph{semisimple}\index{semisimple} if it decomposes as the direct sum of irreducible representations.
\end{definition}

\underline{Exercise:} A representation \(V\) is completely reducible if and only if for every subrepresentation \(W\) of \(V\), there exists another subrepresentation \(W'\) such that
\[V = W \oplus W'\]

\begin{example}
    If \(V\) is a representation, \(W \le V\) a subrepresentation, then the \emph{quotient}\index{quotient representation} \(V/W\) is a representation of \(\mfg\), via
    \[x(v + W) = (xv) + W\]
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    The isomorphism theorems hold for quotient representations as well.
\end{remark}

\section{Representation theory of \(\sl_2(\C)\)}

Recall
\[\sl_2(\C) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}
    a & b \\
    c & -a 
\end{pmatrix}\ \bigg\vert\ a,b,c \in \C\right\}\]

\begin{example}
    We have
    \[\mfb_2 = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}
        a & b \\
        0 & c
    \end{pmatrix}\right\}\]
    which is the Borel subalgebra of \(2 \times 2\) matrices. Let \(V = \Span\left\{v_! = \begin{pmatrix}
        1 \\ 0
    \end{pmatrix}, v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 \\ 1
    \end{pmatrix}\right\}\), and the defining representation of \(\mfb_2\). This is not completely reducible. To see this, set
    \[V_1 = \Span\{v_1\}\]
    and this does not have a complement.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    [faithful]
    A representation \(V\) of \(\mfg\) is \emph{faithful}\index{faithful} if the map
    \[\mfg \to \gl(V)\]
    is injective.
\end{definition}

From now on, all Lie algebras and their representations will be over \(C\), unless stated otherwise.

Let \(V\) be a representation of \(\sl_2(\C)\). Recall the basis
\[e = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \quad f = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \quad h = \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}\]
of \(\sl_2(\C)\). We know three representations of \(\sl_2(\C)\) already.
\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{c c c}
        dimension & name & action of \(h\) \\\hline
        1 & trivial & \(0\) \\
        2 & defining & \(\begin{pmatrix}
            1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1
        \end{pmatrix}\) \\
        3 & adjoint & \(\begin{pmatrix}
            2 \\
            & 0\\
            &&-2
        \end{pmatrix}\)
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{definition}
    [weight space]
    For \(\lambda \in \C\), the \emph{\(\lambda\)-weight space}\index{weight space} of \(V\) is
    \[V_\lambda = \left\{v \in V \mid hv = \lambda v\right\}\]
    is the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(h\).
\end{definition}

The following are \underline{vector space sums}, not decompositions into subrepresentations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item For the trivial representation,
    \[V = V_0\]
    \item For the defining representation,
    \[V = V_1 \oplus V_{-1}\]
    \item For the adjoint representation,
    \[V = V_2 \oplus V_0 \oplus V_{-2}\]
    where \(V_2 = \inner{e}, V_0 = \inner{h}, V_{-2} = \inner{f}\).
\end{itemize}

For \(v \in V_\lambda\),
\[h(ev) = (he)v = ([he] + eh)v = 2ev + \lambda ev = (\lambda + 2)ev\]
Hence \(ev \in V_{\lambda + 2}\). Similarly, \(fv \in V_{\lambda -2 }\).

That is, we have

\begin{definition}
    [highest weight vector]
    A non-zero
    \[v \in V_\lambda \cap \ker(e)\]
    for some \(\lambda\) is called a \emph{highest weight vector (of weight \(\lambda\))}\index{highest weight vector}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    In the adjoint representation, \(e\) is a highest weight vector.
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:e-f-v}
    Suppose \(v \in V_\lambda\) is a highest weight vector. Then for all \(n \ge 1\),
    \[ef^nv = n(\lambda - n + 1)f^{n-1}v\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Induction on \(n\). For \(n = 1\),
    \[(ef)v = ([ef] + fe)v = (h + fe)v = \lambda v = 1(\lambda - 1 + 1)f^0v\]
    The inductive step follows similarly.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:span-f-subrep}
    Suppose \(v \in V_\lambda\) is a highest weight vector. Then
    \[W = \Span\{v, fv, f^2v, \dots\}\]
    is a sub-representation of \(V\),
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Suffices to show that for \(w = f^n v \in W\), then \(ew, fw, hw \in W\). By definition, \(fw \in W\) is obvious. \(ew \in W\) follows by \cref{lem:e-f-v}, and the \(n = 0\) case is just \(ew = 0\).

    For \(hw\),
    \[f^nv \in V_{\lambda - 2n}\]
    and so
    \[hw = (\lambda - 2n)w \in W\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:highest-weight-vector-exists}
    If \(V\) is finite dimensional, then a highest weight vector exists.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Choose any nonzero eigenvector \(v\) of \(h\) (always exists as we are working over \(\C\)). Consider
    \[v, ev, e^2v, \dots\]
    These are eigenvectors for \(h\), with distinct eigenvalues. Hence the set
    \[\{e^nv \mid e^n v \ne 0\}\]
    is linearly independent. As \(V\) is finite dimensional, this set must be finite. Hence there must exists \(n\) such that
    \[e^n v \ne 0 \qqtext{and}e^{n+1}v= 0\]
    Then \(e^nv\) is a highest weight vector.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:highest-weight-integer}
    Suppose \(V\) is finite dimensional, and \(v \in V_\lambda\) is a highest weight vector, then \(\lambda \in \Z_{\ge 0}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Any non-zero vectors of the form \(f^n v\) must be linearly independent, so there exists \(n \ge 0\) such that \(f^n v \ne 0, f^{n+1}v = 0\). By \cref{lem:e-f-v},
    \[0 = ef^{n+1}v = (n+1)(\lambda - n)f^n v\]
    Hence we must have that \(\lambda = n\), since \(n + 1\ne 0, f^n v \ne 0\).
\end{proof}

\underline{Conclusion:} Suppose \(V\) is irreducible, of dimension \(n+1\). Then by \cref{prop:highest-weight-vector-exists}, a highest weight vector \(v \in V_\lambda\) exists. By \cref{lem:span-f-subrep}, we have a subrepresentation
\[W = \Span\{v, fv, \dots\}\]
So by irreducibility,
\[\{v, fv, \dots, f^nv\}\]
is a basis, as the \(f^iv\) are linearly independent, and we know from \cref{lem:highest-weight-integer} that \(\lambda = n\).

\begin{corollary}
    \label{cor:sl2-irrep-actions}
    If \(V\) is an irreducible representation of \(\sl_2\), of dimension \(n+1\), then there exists a basis \(v_0, \dots, v_n\) of \(V\), such that the actions are:
    \[h v_i = (n-2i)v_i \qquad fv_i = \begin{cases}
        v_{i+1} & i+1 \le n \\
        0 & i = n
    \end{cases} \qquad e v_i = \begin{cases}
        i(n-i+1)v_{i-1} & i -1 \ge 0 \\
        0 & i = 0
    \end{cases}\]
    In particular, there is a unique irreducible representation of \(\sl_2\) with dimension \(n+1\) for all \(n \ge 0\).
\end{corollary}

\lec

\begin{remark}
    Let \(V\) be the \(n+1\) dimensional irreducible representation of \(\sl_2\), and let \(v \in V\) be a highest weight vector. Note
    \[\left(ef + fe + \frac{1}{2}h^2\right)(v) = \left(n + \frac{n^2}{2}\right)v\]
\end{remark}

\section{Irreducible modules for \(\sl_2\)}

\begin{notation}
    We will write \(V(n)\) for the \(n+1\) dimensional irreducible representation of \(\sl_2\).
\end{notation}

\begin{definition}
    [weights]
    Given a representation \(V\) for \(\sl_2\),
    \[\{\lambda \in \C \mid V_\lambda \ne 0\}\]
    are the \emph{weights}\index{weights} of \(V\).
\end{definition}

We will show \emph{Weyl's theorem}.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:weyl}
    \index{Weyl's theorem}
    Every finite dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\) is completely reducible.
\end{theorem}

This result, along with \cref{cor:sl2-irrep-actions}, implies that the action of \(h\) completely determines a finite dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\).

\begin{example}
    Suppose \(V\) is a 5-dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\), and there exists \(v \in V\) of weight \(3\). This means that by counting dimensions, the possible weights are \(\{3, 1, -1, -3\}\), and \(\{0\}\). Thus,
    \[V \cong V(3) \oplus V(0)\]
\end{example}

We will need a few facts. Let \(\mfg\) be a Lie algebra, \(\varphi : \mfg \to \gl(V)\) be a representation of \(\mfg\), and suppose there exists \(\sigma\) commuting with \(\varphi(x)\) for all \(x \in \mfg\). Then:

\underline{Fact 1:}
\[\ker(\sigma - \lambda \id_V)\]
is a subrepresentaion of \(V\), for all \(\lambda \in \C\).

\underline{Fact 2:}
If \(V\) is irreducible, then \(\sigma\) is a scalar multiple of \(\id_V\). That is, Schur's Lemma.

\begin{definition}
    [Casimir element]
    Let \(V\) be a finite dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\). Then
    \[\Omega = ef + fe + \frac{1}{2}h^2 \in \gl(V)\]
    is called the \emph{Casimir element (of \(\sl_2\))}\index{Casimir element}.
\end{definition}

In fact, \(\Omega\) is \emph{central}.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:sl2-casimir-central}
    If \(\varphi : \sl_2 \to \gl(V)\) is a representation, then \(\Omega\) commutes with \(\varphi(x)\) for all \(x \in \sl_2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    To show \(\Omega\) is central, suffices to show \(\Omega e = e\Omega, \Omega f = f\Omega, \Omega h = h\Omega\). Just compute.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    If \(V\) is an irreducible finite dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\), then \(\Omega \actson V\) by a scalar.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    By Schur's lemma and \cref{lem:sl2-casimir-central}. Moreover, the scalar is
    \[\frac{n^2}{2} + n\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{thm:weyl}] Let \(\varphi : \sl_2 \to \gl(V)\) be a finite dimensional representation of \(\sl_2\). Let \(W \le V\) be a subrepresentaion. We need to find a subrepresentaion \(U \le V\), such that
    \[V \cong W \oplus U\]

    \textbf{Case 1: \(W\) has codimension \(1\)}. So \(V/W \cong V(0)\).

    \textbf{Subcase (i): \(W\) is trivial.} In this case, \(\dim(V) = 2\), and so we have a basis \(v_1, v_2\) of \(V\), with respect to which \(\sl_2\) acts on \(V\), by matrices
    \[\begin{pmatrix}
        0 & * \\
        0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}\]
    We will show \(V\) is isomorphic to \(\underbrace{V(0)}_{=W} \oplus \underbrace{V(0)}_{=U}\).
    Note that
    \[\left[\begin{pmatrix}
        0 & x \\
        0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & y \\
        0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}\right] = 0\]
    for all \(x, y\). Since \(\varphi\) is a representation, it respects the Lie bracket. We must have that
    \[\varphi(h) = [\varphi(e),\varphi(f)] = 0\]
    and so
    \[\varphi(e) = \frac{1}{2}[\varphi(h), \varphi(e)] = 0\]
    and \(\varphi(f) = 0\) similarly. Thus, the action of \(\sl_2\) is trivial.

    \textbf{Subcase (ii): \(W = V(n)\) is irreducible, \(n > 0\).} Now we have \(\Omega \in \gl(V)\), and we will show
    \[V = V(n) \oplus \ker(\Omega)\]
    By Schur's lemma, and the fact that \(W\) is irreducible, and that \(\Omega\) acts on \(V/W\) trivially, there is a basis for \(V\), such that
    \[\Omega = \begin{pmatrix}
        \lambda I & * \\
        0 & 0 
    \end{pmatrix}\]
    \(W\) is non-trivial by assumption, and so \(\ker(\Omega) \ne 0\), and \(W \cap \ker(\Omega) = 0\). Hence
    \[V = W \oplus \Omega\]

    \textbf{Subcase (iii): For a general \(W\).} We do this by induction on \(\dim(V)\). If \(\dim(V) = 1\), the result is clearly true. So we can assume \(\dim(V) \ge 2\). Let \(W' \le W\) be a non-zero subrepresentation. As \(\dim(W/W') < \dim(V)\), and \(\codim_{V/W'}(W/W') = 1\), by induction, this implies that we have a splitting
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:weyl-decomp-1}
        \frac{V}{W'} = \frac{W}{W'} \oplus \frac{W''}{W'}
    \end{equation}
    for some subspace \(W'' \le V\), with \(W' \sse W''\), and \(W''/W'\) is a subrepresentation of \(V/W'\). Moreover, \(W''/W'\) is codimension \(1\), and \(\dim(W') \le \dim(V)\). \(W''\) being a subrepresentation of \(V\) follows from the fact that \(W''/W'\) is a subrepresentation. By induction again, there exists a subrepresentation \(U \le W''\) such that
    \[W'' = W' \oplus U\]
    We know that
    \[V = W \oplus U\]
    as \(W \cap U = 0\) since \cref{eq:weyl-decomp-1} is a direct sum, and so \(W \cap U \le W' \cap U = 0\). Using \(\dim(U) = 1\), and counting dimensions we are done.

    \textbf{Case 2: Let \(W\) be arbitrary.} Recall the action on \(\Hom(V, W)\), is given by
    \[(x\varphi)(v) = x\varphi(v) - \varphi(xv)\]
    Define
    \[\van = \left\{\psi \in \Hom(V, W) \mid \psi\vert_W = \lambda\id_W \text{ for some }\lambda\right\}\]
    and we have a subspace
    \[\bb W = \left\{\psi \in \van \mid \psi\vert_W = 0\right\} \le \van\]
    Since we lose one degree of freedom going from \(\bb V\) to \(\bb W\), and so \(\codim_{\bb V}(\bb W) = 1\). Suppose \(\psi\vert_W = \lambda \id_W\), \(x \in \sl_2, w\in W\), then
    \[(x\psi)(w) = x\psi(w) - \psi(xw) = x(\lambda w) - \lambda(xw) = 0\]
    So \(\bb V\) is a subrepresentation of \(\Hom(V, W)\), and so \(\bb W\) is a subrepresentation as well. By case 1, there exists a one-dimensional subrepresentation \(\bb U \le \bb V\), such that
    \[\bb V = \bb U \oplus \bb W\]
    Write \(\bb U = \inner{\gamma}\), for some \(\gamma \in \bb V\), and so \(\gamma\vert_W = \lambda \id_W\), for some non-zero \(\lambda\).

    \begin{claim}
        We have a vector space decomposition:
        \[V = W \oplus \ker(\gamma)\]
    \end{claim}

    \lec

    \begin{proof}
        [Proof of claim]
        By construction, \(W \cap \ker(\gamma) = 0\), and by dimension counting, \(\dim(V) = \dim(W) + \dim(\ker(\gamma))\), as \(W = \im(\gamma)\). Since dimensions add up and the intersection is zero, we have a direct sum of vector spaces.
    \end{proof}

    Finally, it remains to show that \(\ker(\gamma)\) is a subrepresentation of \(V\). Let \(v \in \ker(\gamma)\), \(x \in \sl_2\). Since \(\bb U\) is one-dimensional of \(\sl_2\), it must be the trivial representation. Thus,
    \[0 = (x\gamma)(v) = x\gamma(v) - \gamma(x v) = -\gamma(xv)\]
    as \(\gamma(v) = 0\). This means that \(xv \in \ker(\gamma)\) as required.


\end{proof}

In Humphreys' book \S6.3, Humphreys proves \cref{thm:weyl} for a general semisimple Lie algebra. Or see Henderson \S5.2.1, \S7.5.1.

\begin{remark}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The proof only needed (in terms of representation theory)
        \begin{itemize}
            \item existence of a Casimir element \(\Omega\),
            \item the only one-dimensional representation is the trivial representation.
        \end{itemize}
        \item Complete reducibility is rare, it can fail for infinite dimensional Lie algebras, or simple Lie algebras in positive characteristic. For example, the adjoint representation of \(\sl_n(\bb F_p)\) on \(\gl_n(\bb F_p)\) is not completely reducible if \(p \mid n\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{Tensor products}

Given vector spaces \(V, W\), with bases \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) and \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) respectively. We define the \emph{tensor product}\index{tensor product!of vector spaces} \(V \otimes_\C W\) as the \(\C\)-vector space, with basis
\[\{v_i \otimes w_j\}_{i, j}\]
subject to the usual bilinearity conditions.

\begin{definition}
    [tensor product of representations]
    If \(V, W\) are representations of a Lie algebra \(\mfg\), then so is \(V \otimes W\)\index{tensor product!of representations}, with
    \[x(v \otimes w) = (xv) \otimes w + v \otimes (xw)\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    If \(V, W\) are \(\sl_2\) representations, \(v \in V_\lambda, w \in W_\mu\), then
    \[h(v \otimes w) = (\lambda + \mu)(v \otimes w)\]
    That is,
    \[v \otimes w = (V\otimes W)_{\lambda + \mu}\]
    Thus, the weights of \(V \otimes W\) are just \(\lambda + \mu\), where \(\lambda\) is a weight for \(V\), \(\mu\) is a weight for \(W\).  
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    For
    \[V(2) \otimes V(2) = V(2)^{\otimes 2}\]
    we have the weights:
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{c|c c c}
            & 2 & 0 & -2 \\
            \hline
            2 & 4 & 2 & 0 \\
            0 & 2 & 0 & -2 \\
            -2 & 0 & -2 & -4
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    and so
    \[V(2)^{\otimes 2} = V(4) \oplus V(2) \oplus V(0)\]
    In particular, if \(v_n\) is a highest weight vector in \(V(n)\), then \(v_n \otimes v_m\) is a highest weight vector for \(V(n) \otimes V(m)\).
\end{example}

We would like a general formula for decomposing \(V(n) \otimes V(m)\). The answer, as in Part II Representation theory, is a \emph{Clebsch-Gordon}\index{Clebsch-Gordon} formula
\[V(n) \otimes V(m) = \bigoplus_{r = \abs{n - m}, r \cong n - m \pmod 2}^{n + m}V(r)\]
We won't need this though.

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{\(n\)-th symmetric power}\index{symmetric power} is
    \[S^nV = \Sym^n(V) = \frac{V^{\otimes n}}{M_n}\]
    where \(M_n\) is the span of
    \[u_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes u_n - u_{\sigma(1)}\otimes \cdots \otimes u_{\sigma(n)}\]
    where \(\sigma \in S_n\), and \(u_i \in V\).
\end{definition}

For example, \(M_2\) is the span of \(v \otimes w - w \otimes v\). In particular, note that \(M_n\) is a subrepresentation of \(V^{\otimes n}\) of \(V\). So \(\Sym^n(V)\) is a representation of \(V\).

\begin{example}
    In \(S^2V\), \(v \otimes w = w \otimes v\), and so \(S^2 V\) has basis
    \[v_i \otimes v_j\]
    for \(i \le j\). Decomposing \(S^2V(2)\), we see that \(e \otimes e \in S^2V(2)\) is nonzero (note \(V(2)\) is the adjoint representation), and so \(V(4)\) is a subrepresentation of \(S^2V(2)\). In particular, we have a splitting
    \[S^2V(2) = V(4) \oplus V(0)\]
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{\(n\)-th exterior (or alternating) power}\index{exterior power} is
    \[\Lambda^n V = \frac{V^{\otimes n}}{N_n}\]
    where \(N_n\) is the span of
    \[u_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes u_n\]
    where \(u_i \in V\), and \(u_i = u_j\) for some \(i \ne j\).
\end{definition}

Again, \(N_n\) is a subrepresentation of \(V^{\otimes n}\), and so \(\Lambda^n V\) is a representation.

\begin{example}
    With \(n = 2\), \(N_2 = \Span\{v \otimes v\}\).
\end{example}

\begin{notation}
    We write (the coset of) \(u_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes u_n\) in \(\Lambda^n V\) as
    \[u_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge u_n\]
\end{notation}

\underline{Exercises:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Decompose \(\Lambda^2V(2) = V(2)\), with basis \(e \wedge f, e \wedge h, h \wedge f\).
    \item Find the dimensions of \(S^n V\) and \(\Lambda^n V\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results about semisimple Lie algebras}

Let \(\mfg\) be a Lie algebra over \(\C\).

\begin{definition}
    [ideal] A subspace \(I \sse \mfg\) is an \emph{ideal}\index{ideal} of \(\mfg\) if
    \[[xy]\in I\]
    for all \(x \in \mfg, y \in I\).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Any ideal is automatically a subalgebra.
\end{remark}

Suppose \(I\) is an ideal, then \(\mfg/I\) is a Lie algebra under
\[[x + I, y + I] = [x,y] + I\]
Moreover, \(I\) is an ideal if and only if it is a subrepresentation of the adjoint representation \(\ad : \mfg \to \gl(\mfg)\).

\begin{example}
    The \emph{centre}\index{centre} of \(\mfg\) is
    \[Z(\mfg) = \left\{x \in \mfg \mid [x, y] = 0 \text{ for all }y \in \mfg\right\}\]
    and by definition, \(Z(\mfg) = \ker(\ad : \mfg \to \gl(\mfg))\).
\end{example}

If \(Z(\mfg) = 0\), then \(\ad : \mfg \to \gl(\mfg)\) is faithful, and thus we have an embedding
\[\ad : \mfg \to \gl(\mfg)\]
and as such, \(\mfg\) can be regarded as a Lie subalgebra of \(\gl_n\), where \(n = \dim(\mfg)\).

\begin{theorem}
    [Ado] Suppose \(\Char(k) = 0\), then any finite dimensional Lie algebra \(\mfg\) embeds as a Lie subalgebra of \(\gl_m\) for some \(m\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Omitted.
\end{proof}

Note that the embedding need not be via the adjoint representation. In fact, this is true for \(\Char(k) = p > 0\), due to Iwasawa.

\begin{example}
    The \emph{derived subalgebra}\index{derived subalgebra} of \(\mfg\) is
    \[D(\mfg) = [\mfg, \mfg] = \left\{[x, y] \mid x,y \in \mfg\right\}\]
    This is an ideal of \(\mfg\).
\end{example}

Recall \(\sl_n\) is a subalgebra of \(\gl_n\). In fact,
\[D(\gl_n) = \sl_n\]

\begin{example}
    Suppose \(\varphi : \mfg \to \mfh\) is a homomorphism  of Lie algebras, then
    \[\ker(\varphi) = \left\{x \in \mfg \mid \varphi(x) = 0\right\}\]
    is an ideal of \(\mfg\). In fact, every ideal arises in this way.
\end{example}

\lec

\begin{definition}
    [simple] A Lie algebra \(\mfg\) is \emph{simple} if \([\mfg, \mfg] \ne 0\), and the only ideals are \(0\) and \(\mfg\).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    We can show that \(\sl_n\) (for \(n \ge 2\)), \(\so_n\) (for \(n \ge 2\)) and \(\sl_{2\ell}\) (for \(\ell \ge 1\)) are simple.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item if \(\mfg\) is simple, then \([\mfg, \mfg] = \mfg\).
        \item if \(\mfg\) is simple, every representation of \(\mfg\) is either faithful, or the direct sum of trivial representations. 
        \item \(\mfg\) is simple if and only if the adjoint representation is irreducible.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    [semisimple] A Lie algebra \(\mfg\) is \emph{semisimple} if it is the direct sum of simple ideals. That is, ideals which are simple as Lie algebras.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    \[\so_4 \cong \sl_2 \oplus \sl_2\]
\end{example}

We will state a more `standard' definition of semisimple Lie algebras, and show that these are equivalent.

\begin{definition}
    [(lower) central series] The \emph{(lower) central series} of a Lie algebra \(\mfg\) is the sequence of subalgebras
    \[\mfg = \mfg^0 \supseteq \mfg^1 \supseteq \mfg^2 \supseteq \cdots\]
    with \(\mfg^n = [\mfg, \mfg^{n-1}]\). That is,
    \[\mfg \supseteq [\mfg, \mfg] \supseteq [\mfg, [\mfg, \mfg]] \supseteq \cdots\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [derived series, upper central series] The \emph{derived series}, or \emph{upper central series} for a Lie algebra \(\mfg\) is the sequence
    \[\mfg^{(0)} \supseteq \mfg^{(1)} \supseteq \mfg^{(2)} \supseteq \cdots\]
    where \(\mfg^{(0)} = \mfg\), and \(\mfg^{(n)} = [\mfg^{(n-1)}, \mfg^{(n-1)}]\). That is,
    \[\mfg \supseteq [\mfg, \mfg] \supseteq [[\mfg, \mfg], [\mfg, \mfg]] \supseteq \cdots\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\mfg^{(n)} \sse \mfg^n\),
        \item \(\mfg^n\) and \(\mfg^{(n)}\) are ideals.
    \end{itemize}
    To see the second point, we induct on \(n\). The case \(n = 0\) is clear. Let \(x \in \mfg, y \in \mfg^n\). Then
    \[[x, y] \in \mfg^{n-1}\]
    since \(\mfg^{n-1}\) is an ideal, and \(\mfg^n \sse \mfg^{n-1}\). As
    \[\mfg^n = \left\{[x, y] \mid x \in \mfg, y \in \mfg^{n-1}\right\}\]
    this clearly contains \([x, y]\).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item if \(\mfg\) is simple, then \(\mfg^n = \mfg^{(n)} = \mfg\) for all \(n\).
        \item if \(\mfg\) is abelian, then \(\mfg^1 = \mfg^{(1)} = 0\).
        \item let \(\mfn_n \sse \gl_n\) be the Lie algebra of strictly upper triangular \(n \times n\) matrices. The central series for \(\mfn_n\) is
        \[\left\{\begin{pmatrix}
            0 & & * \\
            & \ddots \\
            & & 0
        \end{pmatrix}\right\} \supseteq \left\{\begin{pmatrix}
            0 & 0 & & * \\
            & \ddots & \ddots & \\
            & & \ddots & 0 \\
            & & & 0
        \end{pmatrix}\right\} \supseteq \cdots  \supseteq 0\]
        Note \(\mfn_3\) is the \emph{Heisenberg Lie algebra}.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    [nilpotent] If \(\mfg^n = 0\) for some \(n\), then \(\mfg\) is called \emph{nilpotent}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [solvable, soluble] If \(\mfg^{(n)} = 0\) for some \(n\), then \(\mfg\) is called \emph{solvable} (or \emph{soluble} in BrE).
\end{definition}

\underline{Exercise:} Let \(\mfb_n \sse \gl_n\) be the Borel Lie algebra of upper triangular matrices. Then \(\mfb_n\) is solvable but not nilpotent (for \(n \ge 2\)).

Note on the other hand that as \(\mfg^{(n)} \sse \mfg^n\), nilpotent implies solvable.

The next result allows the theory of complex semisimple Lie algebras to go `far' with minimal work.

\begin{theorem}
    [Lie's theorem] \label{thm:lie}Let \(k = \C\) (or an algebraically closed field with characteristic \(0\)). Let \(\mfg \sse \gl(V)\) be a Lie subalgebra, and suppose \(\mfg\) is solvable. Then there exists a common eigenvector for all elements of \(\mfg\).

    That is, there exists \(v \in V\) non-zero, such that for all \(x \in \mfg\), \(xv = \lambda_xv\) for some \(\lambda_x \in \C\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Omitted. See Humphreys Theorem 4.1.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    There exists a basis for \(V\) such that every element is upper triangular. 
\end{corollary}
In fact,  using \cref{thm:lie} and induction on \(\dim(V)\), we can show that there exists a chain of subspsaces
\[0 = V_0 \le V_1 \le \cdots \le V_n = V\]
with \(\dim(V_i) = i\), and \(\mfg(V_i) \sse V_i\). By considering a basis for \(V_i\), we get the corollary.

Using this result, we can consider \(\mfg \sse \mfb_n\). 

\underline{Aside:} We call the sequence
\[0 = V_0 \le V_1 \le \cdots \le V_n\]
a \emph{maximal flag}, and there is interesting geometry related to this.

\begin{proposition}
    Suppose \(I, J\) are ideals of \(\mfg\). Then
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item if \(\mfg\) is solvable, then any subalgebra or quotient of \(\mfg\) is solvable.
        \item if \(I\) is solvable, and \(\mfg/I\) is solvable, then so is \(\mfg\).
        \item if \(I, J\) are solvable, then so is \(I + J\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    (i) is clear from definitions. For (ii), choose \(n\) such that \((g/I)^{(n)} = 0\). Then this forces \(\mfg^{(n)} \sse I\). But then we have \(\mfg^{(n+m)} \sse I^{(m)}\) for each \(m \ge 0\). Since we know that \(I\) is solvable, we are done.

    For (iii), note that 
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{I + J}{J} \cong \frac{I}{I \cap J}
    \end{align*}
    and the right hand side is solvable, by (i), and \(J\) is solvable by assumption, and so by (ii), \(I + J\) is solvable.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    [radical] The \emph{radical} of \(\mfg\) is \(\Rad(\mfg)\) is the maximal solvable ideal of \(\mfg\). That is, it is the sum of all solvable ideals of \(\mfg\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    [trace form]
    Suppose \(\varphi : \mfg \to \gl(V)\) is a finite dimensional representation of \(\mfg\). Then the \emph{trace form} of \(V\) (or \(\varphi\)) is the symmetric bilinear form
    \begin{align*}
        (\cdot, \cdot) : \mfg \times \mfg &\to \C \\
        (x, y) &= \tr(\varphi(x)\varphi(y))
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}

\underline{Exercise:} We have the invariance relation \(([x, y], z) = (x, [y, z])\)

\begin{definition}
    [Killing form] The \emph{Killing form} \(K(\cdot, \cdot)\) is the trace form of \(\ad\). That is,
    \[K(x, y) = \tr(\ad(x)\ad(y)) = (x, y)_{\ad}\]
\end{definition}

\lec

\begin{theorem}
    [Cartan-Killing criterion] 
    \label{thm:cartan-killing}\index{Cartan-Killing criterion}
    For a finite dimensional Lie algebra \(\mfg\), the following are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(\mfg\) is semisimple,
        \item \(\Rad(\mfg) = 0\),
        \item the Killing form of \(\mfg\) is non-degenerate.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    \(\Rad(\mfg/\Rad(\mfg)) = 0\), since a suitable ideal of \(\mfg/\Rad(\mfg)\) would lift to give an ideal \(J\) of \(\mfg\), containing \(\Rad(\mfg)\), with \(J/\Rad(g)\) solvable. Hence \(J\) is solvable, and \(J \sse \Rad(\mfg)\).

    Using this, \(\mfg/\Rad(\mfg)\) is semisimple.
\end{remark}

\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Let \(\mfg\) be a Lie algebra,
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item if \(I\) is an ideal of \(\mfg\), then so is \([I, I]\),
        \item \(\Rad(\mfg) = 0\) if and only if \(\mfg\) has no non-trivial abelian ideals.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    For (i), if \(x, y \in I, z \in \mfg\), we need to show that
    \[[z,[x,y]] \in [I,I]\]
    Using the Jacobi identity,
    \[[z, [x, y]] = -[x, [y, z]] - [y, [x, z]] \in [I, I]\]
    as \(I\) is an ideal.

    For (ii), it is clear that any abelian ideal is solvable. Conversely, if \(I\) is solvable, then the last non-zero term in the derived series of \(I\) is abelian.
\end{proof}

\begin{notation}
    Define\index{orthogonal, \(\mfg^\perp\)}
    \[\mfg^\perp = \left\{x \in \mfg \mid K(x, y) = 0 \text{ for all }x \in \mfg\right\}\]
\end{notation}

\begin{lemma}
    \(\mfg^\perp\) is an ideal.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    For \(x \in \mfg^\perp, y, z \in \mfg\), then \(K([x, y], z) = K(x, [y, z]) = 0\)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:killing-form-on-ideal}
    Let \(I\) be an ideal of \(\mfg\), and let \(K_I\) denote the Killing form of \(I\). Then
    \[K_I(x, y) = K(x, y)\]
    for all \(x, y \in I\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Choose a basis for \(I\), and extend it to a basis of \(\mfg\). Given \(x, y \in I\), with respect to this basis,
    \[\ad(x) = \begin{pmatrix}
        A & * \\
        0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}\]
    where \(A = (\ad(x))\vert_I\), and similarly for \(\ad(y)\). Set \(B = (\ad(y))\vert_I\). Then
    \[K_I(x, y) = \tr(AB) = \tr(\ad(x)\ad(y)) = K(x, y)\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    [Cartan's criterion (for solvability)] 
    \label{thm:cartan-criterion}
    \index{Cartan's criterion (for solvability)}
    Suppose \(\mfg\) is a subalgebra of \(\gl(V)\), for \(V\) a finite dimensional vector space over \(\C\). If \(K(x, y) = 0\) for all \(x\in \mfg, y \in [\mfg, \mfg]\), then \(\mfg\) is solvable.

    In other words, \(\mfg^{(1)} \le \mfg^\perp\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See Humphreys 4.3 using Lie's theorem and the Jordan decomposition. For the Jordan decomposition, see \S8.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item if \(\mfg = \mfg^\perp\), then \(\mfg\) is solvable.
        \item if \(\mfg\) is simple, then \(\mfg^\perp = 0\),
        \item \(\mfg^\perp\) is solvable for any finite dimensional Lie algebra \(\mfg\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    (i) Consider the adjoint \(\ad : \mfg \to \gl(V)\). The image \(\ad(\mfg) = \mfg/\ker(\mfg) = \mfg/Z(\mfg)\). \(Z(\mfg)\) is solvable, since it is abelian, and by assumption \(\mfg = \mfg^\perp\). By \cref{thm:cartan-criterion}, \(\ad(\mfg)\) is solvable, and so \(\mfg\) is solvable.

    (ii) Since \(\mfg^\perp\) is an ideal, and \(\mfg\) is simple, \(\mfg^\perp = 0\) or \(\mfg^\perp = \mfg\). In the second case, by (i) \(\mfg\) is solvable, contradicting the fact that \(\mfg\) is simple, as \([\mfg, \mfg] = \mfg\).

    (iii) By \cref{lem:killing-form-on-ideal}, \((\mfg^\perp)^\perp = \mfg^\perp\), so by (i), \(\mfg^\perp\) is solvable.
\end{proof}

With this, we can now prove the Cartan-Killing criterion.

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{thm:cartan-killing}]

    First we show (ii) \(\implies\) (iii). In this case, \(\mfg\) is solvable, and so \(\mfg^\perp \le \Rad(\mfg) = 0\). Thus \(K\) is non-degenerate.

    For (iii) \(\implies\) (ii), let \(A\) be an abelian ideal of \(\mfg\). We will show that \(A \sse \mfg^\perp\). Choose \(a \in A, y \in \mfg\). Choose a basis for \(A\), and extend it to a basis of \(\mfg\). That is,
    \[\ad(a) = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & * \\
        0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix} \qqtext{and} \ad(y) = \begin{pmatrix}
        * & * \\
        0 & *
    \end{pmatrix}\]
