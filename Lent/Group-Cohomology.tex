\section{Definitions and resolutions}

Let \(G\) be a group.

\begin{definition}
    [integral group ring]
    The \emph{integral group ring}\index{integral group ring} \(\ZG\) has elements being formal sums
    \[\sum n_g g\]
    for \(n_g \in \Z, g \in G\), and only finitely many \(n_g\) are non-zero. This is a free abelian group under addition, i.e.
    \[\left(\sum m_g g\right) + \left(\sum n_g g\right) = \sum (m_g + n_g) g\]
    and we have multiplication
    \[\left(\sum m_h h\right)\left(\sum n_k k\right) = \sum_g \left(\sum_{hk = g}m_hn_k\right) g\]
\end{definition}

This is the associative ring underlying the integral representation theory of \(G\). We will write \(1 = 1e\) for the multiplicative identity in \(G\).

\begin{definition}
    [module]
    A \emph{(left) \(\ZG\)-module}\index{ZG module@\(\ZG\) module} \(M\) is an abelian group under addition, and a map
    \begin{align*}
        \ZG \times M &\to M \\
        (r, m) &\mapsto rm
    \end{align*}
    such that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(r(m + n) = rm + rn\),
        \item \((r_1 + r_2)m = r_1m + r_2 m\),
        \item \((r_1r_2)m = r_1(r_2m)\),
        \item \(1m = m\)
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A module is \emph{trivial} if \(gm = m\) for all \(g \in G\), \(m \in M\). We say \emph{the trivial module}\index{trivial \(\ZG\) module} is \(\Z\) with trivial action \(gm = m\). A \emph{free \(\ZG\)-module}\index{free \(\ZG\) module} on \(X\) is formal sums
\[\sum r_x x\]
for \(r_x \in \ZG, x \in X\), and finitely many \(r_x\) non-zero. We will write \(\ZG\{X\}\) for this.

\emph{Submodules} and \emph{quotient modules} are defined as we would expect.

\begin{definition}
    [\(\ZG\)-map]
    An \emph{\(\ZG\)-map (or morphism)}\index{\(\ZG\)-map} \(\alpha : M_1 \to M_2\) is a map of abelian groups, with
    \[\alpha(rm_1) = r\alpha(m_1)\]
    for \(r \in \ZG, m_1 \in M_1\).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    The \emph{augmentation map}\index{augmentation map} is
    \begin{align*}
        \epsilon : \ZG &\to \Z \\
        \sum n_g g &\mapsto \sum n_g
    \end{align*}
    where we treat \(\ZG\) as a left \(\ZG\)-module, and \(\Z\) as the trivial module. This is a \(\ZG\) map, and it is also a ring homomorphism.
\end{example}

\begin{notation}
    Write \(\Hom_G(M, N)\)\index{HomGMN@\(\Hom_G(M, N)\)} for the set of \(\ZG\)-maps from \(M \to N\), with pointwise addition.
\end{notation}

\begin{example}
    If we regard \(\ZG\) as a left \(\ZG\)-module, then
    \[\Hom_G(\ZG, M) \cong M\]
    for any left \(\ZG\)-module \(M\), by sending \(\varphi\) to \(\varphi(1)\). This is an isomorphism since
    \[\varphi(r) = \varphi(r1) = r\varphi(1)\]
    Note that \(\Hom_G(\ZG, M)\) is a left \(\ZG\)-module, with
    \[(s\phi)(r) = \phi(rs)\]
    In particular,
    \[\Hom_G(\ZG, \ZG) \cong \ZG\]
    With this, \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi(1)\) corresponds to multiplication on the \emph{right} by \(\varphi(1)\).
\end{example}

Note that \(G\) may not be abelian, so the distinction between left and right modules matters.

\begin{definition}
    If \(f : M_1 \to M_2\) is a \(\ZG\)-map, then the dual map is
    \[f^* : \Hom_G(M_2, N) \to \Hom_G(M_1, N)\]
    for any \(\ZG\)-module \(N\). This is given by
    \[f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f\]

    Similarly, if \(f : N_1 \to N_2\), then we have
    \[f_* : \Hom_G(M, N_1) \to \Hom(M, N_2)\]
    with \(f_*(\varphi) = f \circ \varphi\).
\end{definition}

These are maps of abelian groups, since in general \(\Hom_G(M, N)\) doesn't have to be a \(\ZG\)-module, as we don't have a right action.

\begin{example}
    [Prototypical example] Let \(G = \inner{t}\) be an infinite cyclic group, and consider the graph \(\Gamma\) with vertices \(\{v_i\}_{i \in \Z}\), edges \(v_i \bij v_{i+1}\). Then \(G\) acts on the set \(V\) of vertices, by \(t v_i = v_{i+1}\), and also on the set \(E\) of edges. The action is transitive in both cases. The formal integral sums \(\Z V\) and \(\Z E\) are \(\ZG\)-modules, and are free. Fix the edge \(e : v_0 \bij v_1\). Then we have \(\ZG\)-maps
    \begin{align*}
        \dd : \Z E &\to \Z V \\
        e &\mapsto v_1 - v_0
    \end{align*}
    and also
    \begin{align*}
        \Z V &\to \Z \\
        v_0 &\mapsto 1
    \end{align*}
    This correspond to the augmentation map.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    [chain complex, exact, homology]
    A \emph{chain complex}\index{chain complex} of \(\ZG\)-modules is a sequence

\end{align*}

\begin{lemma}
    Free modules are projective.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(\alpha : M_1 \to M_2\) be a surjective \(\ZG\)-map, \(\beta : \ZG\{X\} \to M_2\). For each \(x \in X\), since \(\alpha\) is surjective, there exists \(m_x \in M_1\) such that \(\alpha(m_x) = \beta(x)\). We can then define \(\overline\beta : \ZG\{X\} \to M_1\), by
    \[\sum r_x \cdot x \mapsto \sum r_x \cdot m_x\]
\end{proof}

From algebraic topology, if we have a connected simplicial complex \(X\), with \(\pi_1(X) = G\), and the universal cover \(\widetilde X\) is contractible, then we have a free resolution of \(\Z\). The point is that the simplicial complex \(X\) contains a lot of information about \(G\). We are trying to replicate this algebraically.

For calculation purposes, we're interested in \emph{small resolutions}. For example, where the free modules have small rank. But for theory development purposes, we're wanting general constructions, and such resolutions tend to be large.

    \(G\) is of type \(\FP_n\) if \(\Z\) has a projective resolution

\begin{example}
    \(G = \inner{t}\) infinite cyclic is of type \(\FP\), \(G = C_n = \inner{t}\) finite cyclic is of type \(\FP_\infty\).

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{standard (or bar) resolution of \(\Z\)}, for any group \(G\), is: Let
    \[G^{(n)} = \{[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_n] \mid g_i \in G\}\]
    be the set of symbols. In particular, \(G^{(0)} = \{[]\}\) Let
    \[F_n = \ZG\{G^{(n)}\}\]
    be the free module on \(G^{(n)}\). The differential is given by
    \begin{align*}
        \dd_n[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_n] &= g_1[g_2\vert\dots\vert g_n] \\
        &- [g_1g_2\vert g_3\vert\dots\vert g_n] \\
        &+ [g_1\vert g_2g_3\vert\dots\vert g_n] + \dots \\
        &+ (-1)^{n-1}[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_{n-1}g_n] \\
        &+ [g_1\vert\dots\vert g_{n-1}]
    \end{align*}
    With this, \(\dd_{n-1} \dd_n = 0\), and we have a chain complex
    % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNyxbMCwwLCJcXGNkb3RzIl0sWzEsMCwiRl9uIl0sWzIsMCwiRl97bi0xfSJdLFszLDAsIlxcY2RvdHMiXSxbNCwwLCJGXzAiXSxbNSwwLCJcXFoiXSxbNiwwLCIwIl0sWzQsNSwiW11cXG1hcHN0byAxIl0sWzAsMV0sWzEsMl0sWzIsM10sWzMsNF0sWzUsNl1d
    \[\begin{tikzcd}
        \cdots & {F_n} & {F_{n-1}} & \cdots & {F_0} & \Z & 0
        \arrow["{[]\mapsto 1}", from=1-5, to=1-6]
        \arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
        \arrow[from=1-2, to=1-3]
        \arrow[from=1-3, to=1-4]
        \arrow[from=1-4, to=1-5]
        \arrow[from=1-6, to=1-7]
    \end{tikzcd}\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    The bar resolution corresponds to the standard resolution in algebraic topology. Consider \(n+1\) tuples \(G^{n+1}\), and form the free abelian group \(\Z G^{n+1}\) under addition. \(G\) acts on \(G^{n+1}\) diagonally, so
    \[g\vdot (h_0, \dots, h_n) = (gh_0, \dots, gh_n)\]
    So \(\Z G^{n+1}\) is a free \(\ZG\)-module, on the basis of \(n+1\) tuples with \(g_0 = 1\). Then we have a correspondence
    \[[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_n] \mapsto (1, g_1, g_1g_2, \dots, g_1\cdots g_n)\]
    Note that removing the first entry gives \(g_1(1, g_2, g_2g_3, \dots, g_2\cdots g_n)\), and removing the seconc entry in the tuple gives \((1, g_1g_2, g_1g_2g_3, \dots, g_1\cdots g_n)\).
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
    The bar resolution is exact.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We'll just consider the \(\dd_n\) as maps of abelian groups. \(F_n\) has basis \(G \times G^{(n)}\) as a free abelian group, and \(G \times G^{(n)}\) is the set
    \[\{g_0[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_n] \mid g_i \in G\}\]
    Define \(\Z\)-linear maps \(s_n : F_n \to F_{n+1}\), such that
    \[\id_{F_n} = \dd_{n+1}s_n + s_{n-1}\dd_n \tag{\(*\)}\]
    given by
    \[s_n(g_0[g_1\vert\dots\vert g_n]) = [g_0\vert\dots\vert g_n]\]
    We can verify that \((*)\) holds on the basis, and so if \(x \in \ker(\dd_{n+1})\), then
    \[x = \id(x) = \dd_{n+1}s_n(x) + s_{n-1}\dd_n(x) = \dd_{n+1}s_n(x) \in \im(\dd_{n+1})\]
    That is, \(s_n\) is a chain homotopy from the identity to zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    A finite group is of type \(\FP_\infty\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    The bar resolution gives such a resolution.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Consider a projective resolution

    We have dropped the \(\Z\)-term in \((\dagger)\).

    Also, these cohomology groups are the homology groups of the chain complex
    \[C_n = \Hom_G(P_{-n}, M)\]
    with \(-\infty < n \le 0\).
\end{remark}

Later, we will see that these cohomology groups are independent of the choice of resolution.

\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    \(\HH^0(M, G) = M^G\) is generally true. But \(\HH^1(M, G) = M_G\) is special behaviour of \(G = \inner{t}\).

    For any group of type \(\FP\), we have that \(\HH^n(G, M) = 0\) for large enough \(n\).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    \(G\) is of \emph{cohomological dimension \(m\) over \(\Z\)} if there exists an \(\ZG\)-module \(M\), with 
    \[\HH^m(G, M) \ne 0\]
    and \(\HH^n(G, M') = 0\) for any \(\ZG\)-module \(M'\), and \(n > m\). 
\end{definition}

Note that for all \(G\), \(\HH^0(G, \Z) = \Z \ne 0\).

\begin{example}
    \(G = \inner{t}\) infinite cyclic is of cohomological dimension \(1\) (over \(\Z\)).

    An exercise is to show that if \(G\) is a free group of finite rank, then it is also of cohomological dimension \(1\). To see this, the Cayley graph is a tree and we can construct a resultion using this.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    The converse is true. A finitely generated group of cohomological dimension \(1\) is free. In fact, this is true in general. See [Stallings, 1968], {Swan, 1969}.
\end{remark}

Now consider the bar resolution in our definition of cohomology. Note that
\[\Hom_G(\ZG\{G^{(n)}\}, M) \cong C^n(G, M) = \left\{\text{functions }G^{(n)} \to M\right\}\]
This is the same as function \(G^n \to M\). We also have that
\[C^0(G, M) = \{\text{functions }[] \to M\} = M\]

\begin{definition}
    The group of \emph{\(n\)-cochains} of \(G\) with coefficients in \(M\) is \(C^n(G, M)\). The \emph{\(n\)-coboundary map}
    \begin{align*}
        \dd^n : C^{n-1}(G, M) &\to C^n(G, M)
    \end{align*}
    is dual to \(\dd_n\) in the bar resolution. For \(\phi \in C^{n-1}(G, M)\),
    \begin{align*}
        (\dd^n\phi)(g_1, \dots, g_n) &= g_1\phi(g_2, \dots, g_n) \\
        &- \phi(g_1g_2, \dots, g_n) \\
        &+\phi(g_1, g_2g_3, \dots, g_n) \\
        &+ \dots \\
        &+(-1)^{n-1}\phi(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{n-1}g_n) \\
        &+(-1)^n\phi(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{n-1})
    \end{align*}
    The group of \emph{\(n\)-cocycles} is \(Z^n(G, M) = \ker(\dd^{n+1}) \sse C^n(G, M)\), and the \emph{\(n\)-coboundaries} is \(B^n(G, M) = \im(\dd^n) \sse C^n(G, M)\).
\end{definition}
Thus,
\[\HH^n(G, M) = \frac{Z^n(G, M)}{B^n(G, M)}\]

\begin{corollary}
    For any group \(G\), \(\HH^0(G, M) = M^G\) is the invariants.
\end{corollary}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{derivation of \(G\) with coefficients in \(M\)}  is a function \(\phi : G \to M\), such that
    \[\phi(gh) = g\phi(h) + \phi(g)\]
\end{definition}
Note that \(Z^1(G, M)\) is the set of derivations. Also note that these are also called `crossed homomorphisms'.

An \emph{inner derivation} is \(\phi\) of the form
\[\phi(g) = gm - m\]
for a fixed \(m \in M\).

\begin{corollary}
    \[\HH^1(G, M) = \frac{\{\text{derivations }G \to M\}}{\{\text{inner derivations} G \to M\}}\]
    In particular, if \(M\) is a trivial \(\ZG\)-module, then
    \[\HH^1(G, M) = \Hom_\Group(G, M)\]
\end{corollary}

\section{Low degree cohomology, group extensions}

\subsection{First cohomology}

Recall that \(\HH^0(G, M) = M^G\) is the group of invariants under \(G\), and that a derivation (or a \(1\)-cocycle) is a map \(\phi : G \to M\), such that
\[\phi(g_1g_2) = g_1\phi(g_2) + \phi(g_1)\]
We will see two interpretations of (inner) derivations. Recall that an inner derivation is one of the form \(\phi(g) = gm - m\), for some fixed \(m \in M\).

Let \(M\) be a \(\ZG\)-module, and consider possible \(\ZG\)-actions on the abelian group \(M \oplus \Z\) of the form
\[g(m, n) = (gm + n\phi(g), n)\]
In this case,
\begin{align*}
    g_1g_2(m, n) = g_1(g_2m + n\phi(g_2), n) = (g_1g_2m + ng_1\phi(g_2) + n\phi(g_1), n)
\end{align*}
On the other hand,
\[(g_1g_2)(m, n) = (g_1g_2 m + n\phi(g_1g_2), n)\]
These are the same exactly when \(\phi\) is a derivation. In particular, if \(M\) is a free \(\Z\)-module of finite rank, then we get a map \(G \to \GL(M)\), by can write this as
\[g \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}
    \theta_1(g) & \dd g \\
    0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}\]
where \(\theta_1(g)\) is the action of \(g\) on \(M\). In particular, this is a homomorphism of groups if and only if \(\phi\) is a derivation. Moreover, \(\phi\) is an inner derivation if and only if \((-m, 1)\) generates a \(\ZG\)-submodule which is a trivial module inside \(M \oplus \Z\).

\lec

For the second interpretation:

\begin{definition}
    [semidirect product]
    Let \(G\) be a group, \(M\) be a \(\ZG\)-module. We can construct their \emph{semidirect product} \(M \rtimes G\) as a group \(M \times G\) with operation
    \[(m_1, g_1) * (m_2, g_2) = (m_1 + g_1m_2, g_1g_2)\]
\end{definition}
Here, we have an isomorphism 
\[M \cong \{(m, 1) \mid m \in M\}\]
This is an abelian normal subgroup. Similarly, we have an isomorphism
\[G \cong \{(0, g) \mid g \in G\}\]
and conjugation by \(\{(0, g)\}\) corresponds to the \(G\)-action on \(M\).

Moreover,
\[\frac{M \rtimes G}{\{(m, 1) \mid m \in M\}} \cong G\]
Note that there is a group homomorphism \(s : G \to M \rtimes G\), \(s(g) = (0, g)\) is such that
\[G \to M \rtimes G \to G\]
is the identity. \(s\) is called a \emph{splitting}. Now if we have another splitting \(s_1 : G \to M \rtimes G\), so that the composition \(G \to M \rtimes G \to G\) is the identity. Define \(\psi_{s_1} : G \to M\), so that
\[s_1(g) = (\psi_{s_1}(g), g)\]
Then \(\psi_{s_1} \in Z^1(G, M)\), and given two splittings \(s_1, s_2\), \(\psi_{s_1} - \psi_{s_2} \in B^1(G, M)\) if and only if there exists an \(m\) such that
\[(m, 1)s_1(g)(m_1)^{-1} = s_2(g)\]
Conversely, given \(\phi \in Z^1(G, M)\), there exists a splitting \(s_1\) such that \(\phi = \psi_{s_1}\).

\begin{theorem}
    \(\HH^1(G, M)\) correspond to the \(M\)-conjugacy classes of splittings.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Examples Sheet 1.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    Let \(G\) be a group, and \(M\) be a \(\ZG\)-module. Then there exists a bijection between \(\HH^2(G, M)\) and the equivalence classes of extensions of \(G\) by \(M\).
\end{theorem}

Given an extenstion
    % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNSxbMCwwLCIwIl0sWzEsMCwiTSJdLFsyLDAsIkUiXSxbMywwLCJHIl0sWzQsMCwiMSJdLFswLDFdLFsxLDJdLFsyLDNdLFszLDRdXQ==
    \[\begin{tikzcd}
        0 & M & E & G & 1
        \arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
        \arrow[from=1-2, to=1-3]
        \arrow[from=1-3, to=1-4]
        \arrow[from=1-4, to=1-5]
    \end{tikzcd}\]
a \emph{section} is a function \(s : G \to E\), such that the composition \(\pi \circ s = \id\), where \(\pi\) is the map \(E \to G\). Note that \(s\) need not be a group homomorphism. Suppose \(s(1) = 1\). Let
\[\phi(g_1,g_2) = s(g_1)s(g_2)s(g_1g_2)^{-1}\]
Then
\[\pi(\phi(g_1g_2)) = 1\]
and so \(\phi(g_1, g_2) \in \ker(\pi) = M\). That is, we have a map \(\phi : G^2 \to M\), which is a \(2\)-cochain. In fact, it is a \(2\)-cocycle.
\begin{align*}
    s(g_1)s(g_2)s(g_2) &= \phi(g_1, g_2)s(g_1g_2)s(g_3) \\
    &= \phi(g_1,g_2)\phi(g_1g_2,g_3)s(g_1g_2g_3) \tag{\(*\)}
\end{align*}
Similarly,
\begin{align*}
    s(g_1)s(g_2)s(g_3) &= s(g_1)\phi(g_2, g_2)s(g_2g_3) \\
    &= s(g_1)\phi(g_2g_3)s(g_1)^{-1}s(g_1)s(g_2g_3) \\
    &= s(g_1)\phi(g_2g_2)s(g_1)^{-1}\phi(g_1, g_2g_3)s(g_1g_2g_3) \tag{\(**\)}
\end{align*}
Equating \((*)\) and \((**)\), cancelling the \(s(g_1g_2g_3)\), and changing into additive notation, we get
\[\phi(g_1, g_2) + \phi(g_1g_2, g_3) = g_1\phi(g_2, g_3) + \phi(g_1, g_2g_3)\]
Hence
\[\dd\phi(g_1, g_2, g_3) = 0\]
and so \(\phi \in Z^2(G, M)\). Note \(\phi\) is \emph{normalised}. That is, \(\phi(1, g) = \phi(g, 1) = 0\). So what we have shown is that an extension of \(G\) by \(M\), along with a choice of section \(s\), yields a normalised 2-cocycle \(\phi \in Z^2(G, M)\).

Now take another choice of section \(s'\), with \(s'(1) = 1\). Then the corresponding normalised cocycles \(\phi\) and \(\phi'\) differ by a coboundary, and so we have defined a map
\[\{\text{extensions}\} \to \HH^2(G, M)\]

\lec

To see this, note that \(\pi(s(g)s'(g)^{-1}) = 1\), and \(\psi(g) = s(g)s'(g)^{-1} \in \ker(\pi) = M\). Thus, we have a map \(\psi : G \to M\). Then
\begin{align*}
    s'(g_1)s'(g_2) &= \psi(g_1)s(g_1)\psi(g_2)s(g_2) \\
    &= \psi(g_1)s(g_1)\psi(g_2)s(g_1)^{-1}s(g_1)s(g_2) \\
    &= \psi(g_1)s(g_1)\psi(g_2)s(g_1)^{-1}\phi(g_1, g_2)s(g_1g_2) \\
    &= \psi(g_1)s(g_1)\psi(g_2)s(g_2)^{-1}\phi(g_1, g_2)\psi(g_1g_2)^{-1}s'(g_1g_2)
\end{align*}
Switching to additive notation:
\begin{align*}
    \phi'(g_1, g_2) &= \psi(g_1) + g_1\psi(g_2) + \phi(g_1, g_2) - \psi(g_1g_2) \\
    &= \phi(g_1, g_2) + \dd\psi(g_1, g_2)
\end{align*}
Thus, \(\phi\) and \(\phi'\) differ by a coboundary. For the rest of the proof, we need:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
    \item to show that equivalent extensions give the same cohomology class,
    \item construct an inverse map from cohomology classes to extensions,
    \item show that the two maps are inverse to each other.
\end{enumerate}
We will show (b), and leave (a) and (c). For this, we need
\begin{lemma}
    Let \(\phi \in Z^2(G, M)\). Then there exists a cochain \(\psi \in C^1(G, M)\), such that \(\phi + \dd\psi\) is a normalised cocycle. Hence every cohomology class can be reresented by a normalised cocycle.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Let \(\psi(g) = -\phi(1, g)\). Then
    \[(\phi + \dd\psi)(1, g) = \phi(1, g) - \left(\phi(1, g) - \phi(1, g) + \phi(1, 1)\right) = \phi(1, g) - \phi(1, 1)\]
    and
    \[(\phi + \dd\psi)(g, 1) = \phi(g, 1) - g\phi(1, 1)\]
    But we know that \(\dd\phi(1, 1, g) = \dd\phi(g, 1, 1) = 0\) as \(\phi\) is a cocycle. A computation shows that both of the above are zero.
\end{proof}

Now take a normalised cocycle \(\phi\) representating our cohomology class. We construct an extension as follows.

	\arrow[from=1-3, to=1-4]
	\arrow[from=1-4, to=1-5]

by considering \(E = M \times G\), with the product structure
\[(m_1, g_1)(m_2, g_2) = (m_1 + g_1m_2 + \phi(g_1, g_2), g_1g_2)\]
For this to be a group operation, we use that \(\phi\) is normalised. In this case, we do have an extension, where the map \(\pi\) is projection onto the second factor. Notice if we have another normalised \(2\)-cocycle \(\phi'\) representing our cohomology class, then \(\phi - \phi' = \dd\psi\) is a coboundary, and we can define a map

    (m, g) &\mapsto (m + \psi(g), g)
\end{align*}
This gives us an equivalence of extensions.

Another approach to consider (central) extensions is to use a partial resolution arising from generators and relations.

There is also a connection with homology groups. Given a projective resolution of the trivial \(\ZG\)-module \(\Z\), instead of applying \(\Hom(\cdot, M)\), we can apply the functor \(\Z \otimes_{\ZG} \cdot\). We get a chain complex, and we can define the associated homology groups.

The homology groups don't depend on the choice of resolution. We will write the homology groups as \(\HH_n(G; \Z)\).

	\arrow[from=1-2, to=1-3]
	\arrow[from=1-3, to=1-4]
	\arrow[from=1-4, to=1-5]
\end{tikzcd}\]
    where \(\Ext^1(G_\ab, M)\) arises by applying \(\Hom_\Z(\cdot, M)\) to a projective resolution of an abelian group \(G_\ab\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
    Suppose \(G = [G, G]\). Then \(G_\ab = 1\), and we get that
    \[\HH^2(G, M) \cong \Hom(\mathrm{M}(G), M)\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    Sometimes people define the Schur multiplier to be \(\HH^2(G, \C^*)\) instead of \(\HH_2(G; \Z)\). Schur was considering projective representations, \(G \to \mathrm{PGL}(\C)\). Such a map lifts to a linear representation of a central extension of \(G\).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}

    Given a presentation \(G = F/R\),

    Note that this is not necessarily all of \(F/[R, F]\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    This shows that \(([F, F] \cap R)/[R, F]\) is independent of the choice of presentation.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:ideal-props}
    Let \(G\) be a group, and \(M\) is a (left) \(\ZG\)-module. Then
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item \(I_G = \ker(\epsilon : \ZG \to \Z)\) is a free abelian group under addition, on the basis \(\{g - 1 \mid g \in G \setminus 1\}\),
        \item \(I_G / I_G^2 \cong G_\ab\),
        \item \(\Der(G, M) \cong \Hom_G(I_G, M)\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    For (i), the kernel of \(\epsilon : \ZG \to \Z\) are of the form
    \[\sum n_g g\]
    where
    \[\sum n_g = 0\]
    In this case, we can write
    \[\sum n_g g = \sum n_g (g - 1) + \sum n_g = \sum n_g (g - 1)\]
    Clearly anything of this form is in the kernel of \(\epsilon\). Also, the set \(\{g - 1 \mid g \in G \setminus 1\}\) forms a basis, since if
    \[\sum n_g (g - 1) = 0\]
    Then by reading off the coefficient of \(g\), we see that \(n_g = 0\).

    For (ii), define a group homomorphism
    \begin{align*}
        \theta : I_G &\to G_\ab \\
        g - 1 &\mapsto g[G, G]
    \end{align*}
    But \((g_1 - 1)(g_2 - 1) = (g_1g_2 - 1) - (g_1 - 1) - (g_2 - 1)\), and so \(I_G^2 \sse \ker(\theta)\). Hence we have a natural map \(I_G/I_G^2 \to G_\ab\).

    Conversely, define \(\phi : G \to I_G/I_G^2\), by sending \(g \to (g - 1) + I_G^2\). This is a group homomorphism. Since \(I_G/I_G^2\) is abelian, we have an induced map \(\overline\phi : G_\ab \to I_G/I_G^2\). Then we can check that these two maps define inverses.

    For (iii), the map sends a derivation \(\phi\) to \(\theta \in \Hom_G(I_G, M)\), where \(\theta(g-1) = \phi(g)\). We can check that \(\theta\) is a \(\ZG\)-map. Conversely, given \(\theta \in \Hom_G(I_G, M)\), define \(\phi\) by \(\phi(g) = \theta(g - 1)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item Let \(F\) be a gree group on \(X\). Then \(I_F\) is a free \(\Z F\)-module, on \(\widetilde X = \{x - 1 \mid x \in X\}\).
        \item If \(R\) is a normal subgroup of \(F\), then \(R\) is free on a set \(Y\), then \(\overline{I_R}\) is a free (left) \(\Z F\)-module on \(\widetilde Y = \{y - 1 \mid y \in Y\}\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    For (i), let \(\alpha : \widetilde X \to M\) be a function, to a \(\Z F\)-module \(M\). By definition of freeness, it suffices to show that \(\alpha\) extends to a \(\Z F\)-map \(I_F \to M\).

    First let \(\alpha' : F \to M \rtimes F\), defined by \(\alpha'(x) = (\alpha(x - 1), x)\). This is a group homomorphism as \(F\) is free. Thus for each \(f \in F\), \(\alpha'(f) = (a, f)\) for some \(a \in M\). Then we have a function \(\overline{\alpha} : F \to M\), sending \(f\) to \(a\). Hence
    \[\alpha'(f) = (\overline{\alpha}(f), f)\]
    Note that 
    \begin{align*}
        \alpha(f_1f_2) &= \alpha(f_1)\alpha(f_2) \\
        &= (\overline{\alpha}(f_1), f_1)(\overline{\alpha}(f_2), f_2) \\
        &= (\overline{\alpha}(f_1) + f_1\overline{\alpha}(f_2), f_1f_2)
    \end{align*}
    Hence \(\overline{\alpha}(f_1f_2) = \overline{\alpha}(f_1) + f_1\overline{\alpha}(f_2)\). Hence \(\overline{\alpha}\) is a derivation \(F \to M\). By the previous lemma, we have a corresponding \(\Z F\)-map \(I_F \to M\).

    For (ii), suppose
    \[\sum r_y (y - 1) = 0\]
    with \(r_y \in \Z F\). Choose a transversal \(T\) to the cosets of \(R\) in \(F\). We can write
    \[r_y = \sum_{t \in T} ts_{t, y}\]
    with \(s_{t, y} \in \Z R\). So
    \[\sum_{t \in T}\sum_{y \in Y}t s_{t, y}(y - 1) = 0\]
    Since \(\Z F\) is free abelian, for each \(t\),
    \[\sum_{y \in Y} s_{t, y}(y - 1) = 0\]
    
    But \(I_R\) is a free \(\Z R\)-module on \(\{y - 1 \mid y \in Y\}\) by (i), and so \(s_{t, y} = 0\) for all \(y \in Y, t \in T\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}

\end{proof}

\section{Brauer groups and Galois cohomology}

\begin{definition}
    [central simple algebra]
    A \emph{central simple algebra \(A\)} over a field \(k\) is a finite dimensional \(k\)-vector space, with associative multiplication. Moreover, \(Z(A) = k\) and the only two-sided ideals are \(0\) and \(A\). 
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    \(\Mat_n(k)\) is a central simple algebra.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    Let \(k = \R\). Then the quaternions \(\bb H\) is a central simple algebra. This is in fact a division ring, any non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.
\end{example}

We would like to classify central simple \(k\)-algebras, for a field \(k\).

\begin{theorem}
    [Artin-Wedderburn] 
    \label{thm:artin-wedderburn}
    A finite dimensional simple \(k\)-algebra \(A\) is isomorphic to \(\Mat_n(D)\), where \(D\) is a division ring over \(k\).
\end{theorem}

We will prove this later.

\begin{remark}
    Note that \(Z(A) = \{\lambda I \mid \lambda \in Z(D)\}\).
\end{remark}

We will define an equivalence relation on central simple \(k\)-algebras: We say that \(A \sim B\) if
\[A\otimes_k \Mat_n(k) \cong B \otimes_k \Mat_m(k)\]
for some \(m, n\). From Artin-Wedderburn, \(A \cong M_n(D)\), and so \([A] = [D]\).

\begin{definition}
    [Brauer group]
    The \emph{Brauer group} \(\Br(k)\) is the set of equivalence classes, with
    \[[A][B] = [A \otimes_k B]\]
\end{definition}

Recall that
\[(a_1\otimes b_1)(a_2 \otimes b_2) = (a_1a_2)\otimes (b_1b_2)\]
This has identity element
\[[k] = [\Mat_n(k)]\]
and
\[[A]^{-1} = [A^\mathrm{op}]\]

\lec

\begin{definition}
    [opposite ring]
    \(A^\mathrm{op}\) is the \(k\)-algebra with the same underlying \(k\)-vector space, but with
    \[a \cdot_\mathrm{op} b = b \cdot a\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    A right (resp. left) \(A\)-module is the same as a left (resp. right) \(A^\mathrm{op}\)-module.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
    \(A \otimes A^\mathrm{op} \cong \Mat_n(k)\), where \(n = \dim_k(A)\).
\end{lemma}

We will prove this later.

\begin{example}
    If \(k\) is algebraically closed, \(\Br(k) = 1\) since any division algebra \(D\) which is finite dimensional over \(k\) is \(k\) itself, since any non-zero element is algebraic over \(k\), and so in \(k\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    \(\Br(\R) = \{[\R], [\bb H]\}\).
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    The subgroup \(\Br(L/k)\) of \(\Br(k)\) is the group of equivalence classes represented by a central simple \(k\)-algebra \(A\), such that \(A \otimes_K L \cong \Mat_n(L)\) for some \(n\), and \(L/k\) is (finite) Galois. In this case, we say that \(A\) is \emph{split by \(L\)}. 
\end{definition}

We will prove

\begin{theorem}
    There exists a homomorphism of abelian groups
    \[\HH^2(\Gal(L/k), L^\times) \to \Br(L/k)\]
    which sends \([\phi]\) to \([A(L, G, \phi)]\), where \(G = \Gal(L/k)\). \(A(L, G, \phi)\) is a crossed product which we will construct.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    In fact, this homomorphism is an isomorphism.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    We have a directed union
    \[\Br(k) = \bigcup_{L/k \text{finite Galois}}\Br(L/k)\]
    and so we can express \(\Br(k)\) as a directed union of second cohomology groups.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    When \(k = \R\), we know that there are only two Galois extensions, namely \(\R\) and \(\C\). We know \(\Gal(\C/\R)\) is cyclic of order 2, generated by complex conjugation. In this case, \(\HH^2(G, \C^*) = \Z/2\Z\), generated by \(\phi\) say.

    In our prototypical example, \(\bb H = A(\C, C_2, \phi)\). In this case, we can write
    \[\C = \R \oplus \R i \sse \bb H \oplus \C \oplus \C j\]
    \(\C\) is a maximal subfield of \(\bb H\), and we have a basis \(e_1 = 1, e_\phi = j\) over \(\C\).

    We have a map \(\phi : G \times G \to \C^*\), given by
    \[e_\sigma e_\tau = \phi(\sigma, \tau)e_{\sigma\tau}\]
    Here, \(\phi(\sigma, \tau) \in \C^*\) and \(\phi\) is a normalised \(2\)-cocycle.
\end{example}

Our general construction will start from a Galois extension \(L/k\), and constructing an algebra with a \(2\)-cocycle telling us how to multiply basis elements.

Before that, we need to prove some things:

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{thm:artin-wedderburn}]
    Consider the sum \(I\) of the minimal non-zero right \(A\)-submodules of \(A_A\), which is \(A\) thought of as a right \(A\)-module.
    
    Thus, \(I\) is a sum of simple right \(A\)-modules. 
    
    Let \(M\) be a simple right \(A\)-submodule of \(A_A\), consider the map
    \begin{align*}
        \theta_a : M &\to aM \\
        m &\mapsto am
    \end{align*}
    \(\theta_a\) is a right \(A\)-module map. Since \(M\) is simple, \(\theta_a\) is zero or injective. Thus, \(aM\) is either zero or itself a simple submodule of \(A_A\), isomorphic to \(M\).

    Now consider
    \[\sum_{a \in A}aM\]
    This is a two-sided ideal of \(A\), and it is the sum of simple right \(A\)-modules isomorphic to \(M\). Thus,
    \[A_A = \sum_{a \in A}aM\]

    We need

    \begin{lemma}
        [Schur's lemma] \(\End_A(M) \cong D\) is a division algebra, for a simple right \(A\)-module \(M\).
    \end{lemma}

    \begin{proof}
        Any module map \(\phi : M \to M\) is either zero or an isomorphism, by considering the options of \(\ker(\phi)\) and \(\im(\phi)\). Thus, any non-zero element of \(\End_A(M)\) is invertible.
    \end{proof}

    Now \(A_A\) is a sum of simple right \(A\)-submodules, isomorphic to \(M\). An easy induction shows that we may take \(A_A\) to be a direct sum. Thus,
    \[A_A = \bigoplus_i M_i\]
    where \(M_i \cong M\). Now
    \[\End_A(A_A) \cong \Mat_n(\End_A(M))\]
    But as \(k\)-algebras, \(A \cong \End_A(A_A)\), since an endomorphism is determined by the image of \(1\). Thus, \(A \cong \Mat_n(D)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    Every finitely generated right \(A\) module \(V\) is isomorphic to a direct sum of copies of \(M\) (as above), and any two \(A\)-modules with the same dimension are isomorphic.

    Moreover, \(\End_A(V) \cong \Mat_r(D)\) for some \(r\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(M\) be a simple submodule of \(A_A\), \(v_1, \dots, v_s\) be a set of generators of \(V\) as a right \(A\)-module. Then the map
    \[(a_1, \dots, a_s) \mapsto \sum_i a_iv_i\]
    shows \(V\) as a quotient of a sum of copies of \(A_A\). But \(A_A\) is a direct sum of copues of \(M\), and so \(V\) is a quotient of a direct sum of copies of \(M\).

    Induction shows that \(V \cong \oplus_i M_i\), where \(M_i\) is isomorphic to \(M\). In particular,
    \[\End_A(V) \cong \Mat_r(D)\]
    where \(D = \End_A(M)\) and the dimension of \(V\) as a \(k\)-vector space determines \(r\).
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Let \(V\) be a finite dimensional \(k\)-vector space, with basis \(\{e_i\}_{i \in I}\). For \(v \in V\), define its \emph{support}
    \[J(v) = \{i \in I \mid a_i \ne 0\}\]
    where
    \[v = \sum_i a_i e_i\]

    For \(W\) a subspace of \(V\), \(w \in W\) non-zero is \emph{primordial} with respect to the basis if
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \(J(w)\) is minimal amongst \(\{J(w') \mid w' \in W\}\),
        \item \(a_i = 1\) for some \(i\), when \(w = \sum a_i e_i\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item For \(w, w' \in W\), with \(J(w)\) minimal, \(J(w') \sse J(w)\) if and only if \(w' = cw\) for some \(c \in k\). If so, \(J(w) = J(w')\).
        \item The primordial elements span \(W\).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    (i) is clear. For (ii), induct on \(\abs{J(w)}\). Let
    \[w = \abs{i \in J(w)}a_ie_i\]
    Amongst non-zero elements \(w' \in W\) with \(J(w') \sse J(w)\), choose one with \(\abs{J(w')}\) minimal. Then \(w_0 = cw'\) for some \(c \in k^\times\) is primordial. Now
    \[w = a_jw_0 + (w - a_jw_0)\]
    with \(w - a_jw_0 \in W\), and \(\abs{J(w - a_jw_0)} < \abs{J(w)}\).
    
    By the inductive hypothesis, \(w - a_jw_0\) is a linear combination of primordial elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    All of the same applies for vector spaces over division algebras.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
    Let \(A\) be a \(k\)-algebra, \(D\) a division algebra over \(k\), with centre \(k\). Then every two-sided ideal \(I\) of \(A \otimes_k D\) is generated as a left \(D\)-module by \(I \cap (A \otimes 1)\). 
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The left \(D\)-module structure on \(A \otimes_k D\) is given by
    \[\delta(a \otimes \delta') = a \otimes (\delta\delta')\]
    The ideal \(I\) is a \(D\)-submodule of \(A \otimes_k D\). 
    
    Let \(\{e_i\}_{i \in I}\) be a basis for \(A\) as a \(k\)-vector space, then \(\{e_i \otimes 1\}_{i \in I}\) is a basis for \(A \otimes_k D\) as a left \(D\)-module, Let \(r \in I\) be primordial with respect to this basis, say
    \[r = \sum_{i \in J(r)}\delta_i(e_i \otimes 1) = \sum_{i \in J(r)} e_i \otimes \delta_i\]

    For any non-zero \(\delta \in D\), \(r\delta \in I\), and 
    \[r\delta = \sum_i \delta_i\delta(e_i \otimes 1)\]
    In particular, \(J(r\delta) = J(r)\), and so \(r\delta = \delta' r\) for some \(\delta' \in D\).

    Since \(r\) is primordial, we have that \(\delta_i = 1\) for some \(i\), \(\delta = \delta'\), and so each \(\delta_i \in Z(D) = k\), and so \(r \in A \otimes 1\). Hence every primordial element of \(I\) is in \(A \otimes 1\). But by the previous lemma, the primordial elements span.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    The tensor product of two finite dimensional simple \(k\)-algebras, at least one of whicb is central, is simple.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    By Artin-Wedderburn, we may assume that one of the algebras is \(\Mat_n(D)\), where \(D\) is a division ring over \(k\) and central. Let \(A\) be the other simple algebra. By the lemma, \(A \otimes_k D\) is simple. So by Artin-Wedderburn, \(A \otimes_k D \cong \Mat_{n'}(D')\) for some division algebra \(D'\) over \(k\), and thus,
    \[A \otimes_k \Mat_n(D) \cong \Mat_n(A \otimes_k D) \cong \Mat_n(\Mat_{n'}(D')) = \Mat_{nn'}(D')\]
    and this is simple.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    The tensor product of two central simple \(k\)-algebras is a central simple \(k\)-algebra.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Use the proposition, along with the fact that
    \[Z(A \otimes_k B) = Z(A) \otimes_k Z(B)\]
\end{proof}

    a \otimes a' &\mapsto (v \mapsto ava')
\end{align*}
Here, \(V\) is the vector space underlying \(A\) and \(A^\opp\). The map is injective, since \(A \otimes A^{\opp}\) is simple, and \(1 \otimes 1\) is mapped to the identity. By dimension counting, it is a linear isomorphism.

\begin{definition}
    Let \(G = \Gal(L/k)\), where \(L/k\) is a finite Galois extension, and \(\phi : G \times G \to L^\times\) is a normalised \(2\)-cocycle. We will define \(A = A(L, G, \phi)\).

    As a \(L\)-vector space,  let \(A\) have basis \(\{e_\sigma\}_{\sigma \in G}\). The multiplication is 
    \begin{align*}
        \left(\sum_{\sigma \in G} \lambda_\sigma e_\sigma\right)\left(\sum_{\tau \in G} \mu_\tau e_\tau\right) &= \sum_{\sigma, \tau \in G}\lambda_\sigma \sigma(\mu_\tau)\phi(\sigma, \tau)e_{\sigma, \tau}
    \end{align*}
    This has multiplciative identity \(e_1\), since \(\phi\) is normalised. The multiplication is associative, since \(\phi\) is a \(2\)-cocycle.
\end{definition}

The centre of \(A(L, G, \phi)\) is \(k\). To see this, assume
\[x = \sum\lambda_\sigma e_\sigma \in Z(A)\]
Then for \(\beta \in L\), \((\beta e_1)x = x(\beta e_1)\). That is,
\[\sum_\sigma (\beta \lambda_\sigma)e_\sigma = \sum_\sigma \lambda_\sigma \sigma(\beta)e_\sigma\]
Thus, \(\beta = \sigma(\beta)\) for any \(\beta \in L, \sigma \in G\) with \(\lambda_\sigma \ne 0\). Thus, \(\lambda_\sigma = 0\) for \(\sigma \ne 1\), and hence \(x = \lambda_1 e_1\). But this has to commute with all \(e_\tau\), and so
\[Z(A) = ke_1 \cong k\]

Next, we note that \(A\) is simple. Let \(I\) be a two-sided dieal, and \(x \in I\) non-zero. Say
\[x = \lambda_{\sigma_1}e_{\sigma_1} + \cdots + \lambda_{\sigma_m}e_{\sigma_m}\]
with \(m\) minimal\footnote{We choose \(x\) such that \(m\) is minimal.}. If \(m > 1\), we can find \(\beta \in L^\times\) such that \(\sigma_m(\beta) \ne \sigma_{m-1}(\beta)\). Then
\[y = x - \sigma_m(\beta)x\beta^{-1} \in I\]
The coefficient of \(e_{\sigma_m}\) in \(y\) is zero, and this contradicts minimality. So \(x = \lambda_\sigma e_\sigma\) for some \(\sigma \in G\). This is a unit, and has inverse \(x^{-1} = \sigma^{-1}(\lambda_\sigma^{-1})e_{\sigma^{-1}}\). Hence \(I = A\).

Suppose \([\phi] = [\phi']\), where \(\phi, \phi'\) are normalised \(2\)-cocycles. Then \(\phi, \phi'\) differ by a coboundary. So
\[\phi'(\sigma, \tau) = \phi(\sigma, \tau)\sigma(u_\tau)u_{\sigma\tau}^{-1}u_\sigma\]
where we write things multiplicatively, and \(u : G \to L^\times\). Define a \(L\)-linear map
\begin{align*}
    F : A(L, G, \phi') &\to A(L, G, \phi) \\
    F(e_\sigma') &= u_\sigma e_\sigma
\end{align*}
With this,
\begin{align*}
    F(e_\sigma')F(e_\tau') = F(e_\sigma'e_\tau')
\end{align*}
and so \(F\) is an algebra homomorphism \(A(L, G, \phi') \to A(L, G, \phi)\). But these are simple algebras, and the equality of dimension on both sides means that \(F\) is an isomorphism.

Thus, the map \(\HH^2(G, L^\times) \to \Br(k)\) only depends on the cohomology class. Recall that every cohomology class is represented by a(t least one) normalised cocycle. It remains to show our map
\[\HH^2(\Gal(L/k), L^\times) \to \Br(k)\]
is a homomorphism of abelian groups.

\begin{lemma}
    For normalised \(2\)-cocycles \(\phi, \phi'\),
    \[A(L, G, \phi + \phi') \sim A(L, G, \phi) \otimes A(L, G, \phi')\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(A = A(L, G, \phi), B = A(L, G, \phi'), C = A(L, G, \phi + \phi')\). Regard \(A, B\) as left \(L\)-vector spaces. Define \(V = A \otimes_L B\). Note that
    \[V = \frac{A \otimes_k B}{\inner{(la) \otimes b - a \otimes (lb) \mid a \in A, b \in B, l \in L}}\]
    \(V\) has a right \(A \otimes_k B\)-structure, given by
    \[(a' \otimes_L b')(a \otimes_k b) = (a'a) \otimes_L (b'b)\]
    and \(V\) has a left \(C\)-module structure,
    \[(le_\sigma'')(a \otimes_L b) = (le_\sigma a) \otimes_L (e'_\sigma b)\]
    where \((e_\sigma)\) is a basis of \(A\), \((e'_\sigma)\) is a basis of \(B\), and \((e_\sigma'')\) is a basis of \(C\).

    The two actions commute, and so the right action of \(A \otimes_k B\) on \(V\) defines a homomorphism
    \[f : (A \otimes_k B)^\opp \to \End_C(V)\]
    Note here we need the opposite ring since we think of \(\End_C\) as endomorphisms as a left-module. \(f\) is injective, since \(A \otimes_k B\) is simple.

    \begin{claim}
        The two algebras have the same dimension, and so \(f\) is an isomorphism.
    \end{claim}

    We'll assume this for now. Then note that
    \[\End_C(V) \sim C^{\opp}\]
    since we swap from a left \(C\)-module to a right \(C^{\opp}\)-module. Thus,
    \[(A \otimes_k B)^\opp \sim C^\opp\]
    and so \(A \otimes_k B \sim C\), and \([A][B] = [A \otimes_k B] = [C]\) in \(\Br(k)\).

    \begin{proof}
        [Proof of claim]
        We know that \(C\) is a simple algebra, and so \(C^\opp\) is as well. \(V\) is a left \(C\)-module, and so it is a right \(C^\opp\)-module. Moreover,
        \[V \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^r M\]
        where \(M\) is a simple \(C^{\opp}\)-module. Thus,
        \[\End_C(V) \cong \End_{C^\opp}(V) \cong \Mat_r(D)\]
        for some division algebra \(D = \End_{C^{\opp}}(M)\). But
        \[C^{\opp} \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^m M\]
        by Artin-Wedderburn, which in turn is isomorphic to \(\Mat_m(D)\), with \(\dim(M) = m\dim(D)\).
        
        Now consider dimensions,
        \begin{align*}
            \dim(V) &= \dim(M) \\
            \dim(C) &= \dim(C^\opp) = m^2\dim(D) \\
            \dim(\End_C(V)) &= r^2\dim(D)
        \end{align*}
        and so
        \[\dim(\End_C(V))\dim(C) = \dim(V)^2\]
    \end{proof}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cup product}

\begin{definition}
    [cup product]
    Given \([u] \in \HH^p(G, M), [v] \in \HH^q(G, N)\), define their \emph{cup product} \([u] \smile [v] = [u \smile v] \in \HH^{p+q}(G, M \otimes_\Z N)\) by defining it on cochains:

    The \emph{diagonal action} of \(G\) on \(M \otimes_\Z N\) is given by
    \[g(m \otimes n) = (gm) \otimes (gn)\]

    Let \(u \in C^p(G, M), v \in C^q(G, N)\), define \(u \smile v \in C^p(G, M \otimes N)\) by
    \[(u \smile v)(g_1, \dots, g_{p+q}) = (-1)^{pq}u(g_1, \dots, g_p) \otimes \left(g_1\cdots g_pv(g_{p+1}, \dots, g_{p+q})\right)\]
    This induces a cup product on cohomology classes.
\end{definition}

In degree zero,
\[\HH^0(G, M) \to \HH^0(G, N) \to \HH^0(G, M \otimes N)\]
corresponds the map \(M^G \otimes N^G \to (M \otimes N)^G\) induced by inclusions.

\lec

Moreover, there exists \(1 \in \HH^0(G, \Z)\) which is the unit for the cup product. For associativity, if we have \(u_i \in \HH^*(G, M_i)\), then
\[(u_1 \smile u_2) \smile u_3 = u_1 \smile (u_2 \smile u_3) \in \HH^*(G, M_1 \otimes M_2 \otimes M_3)\]
In addition, this is graded commutative. For \(u \in \HH^p(G, M), v \in \HH^q(G, N)\), then
\[u \smile v = (-1)^{pq}\alpha_*(v \smile u)\]
where \(\alpha : M \otimes N \to N \otimes M\) is the canonical isomorphism. This means that \(\HH^*(G, \Z)\) is graded commutative.

\begin{remark}
    Graded commutative does not imply commutative. But if we restrict outselves to cohomology of even degree only, then it is commutative.
\end{remark}

Moreover, we have a natural graded \(\HH^*(G, \Z)\)-module structure on \(\HH^*(G, M)\).

Now suppose \(\alpha : H \to G\) is a group homomorphism. Then \(\alpha^*(u \smile v) = \alpha^*u \smile \alpha^*v\), for \(u \in \HH^*(G, M)\) and \(v \in \HH^*(G, N)\). Thus,
\[\alpha^* : \HH^*(G, \Z) \to \HH^*(H, \Z)\]
is a ring homomorphism.

\begin{remark}
    Recall that we can take a projective resolution of \(M\), and apply \(\Hom_G(\cdot, N)\), for \(\ZG\)-modules \(M, N\), to get \(\Ext^n(M, N)\). Taking \(M = N\), we get \(\Ext^n(M, M)\), which is a graded \(\HH^*(G, \Z)\)-module. So we can study modules \(M\) by considering these modules for \(\HH^*(G, \Z)\).

    See Benson-Carlson.
\end{remark}

\section{Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence}

Let \(G\) be a group, \(H\) be a normal subgroup, \(Q = G/H\). We would like to calculate the cohomology of \(G\) from that for \(H\) and \(Q\). In low degree, we get the \emph{five term exact sequence}. We use a general method for calculating (co)homology of double complexes using filtrations. 

For the Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence, we have a particular double complex. Let \(X^\bullet\) be a \(\ZG\)-projective resolution of \(\Z\). amd \(Y^\bullet\) be a \(\Z Q\)-projective resolution of \(\Z\). Then \(X^\bullet\) is also a \(\Z H\)-projective resolution of \(\Z\). Let \(M\) be a \(\ZG\)-module. Then \(G\) acts on \(\Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)\) by
\[(gf)(x) = g(f(g^{-1}x))\]
Since \(H\) acts trivially under the above action, we may view \(\Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)\) as a \(\Z Q\)-module. We then form a double complex
\[A = \Hom_Q(Y^\bullet, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M))\]
with differentials
\begin{align*}
    \dd' &= \Hom_Q(\dd_y, \id) \\
    \dd'' &= \Hom_Q(\id, \dd_X^*)
\end{align*}
In this case, \(A^{p,q}\) is zero outside of the first quadrant. In general, we have a double complex \(A^{p, q}\), and differentials \(\dd', \dd''\) of degree \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) respectively. Here, we require
\[(\dd')^2 = (\dd'')^2 = 0\]
and
\[\dd' \dd'' + \dd'' \dd' = 0\]
Thus,  if we set \(\dd = \dd' + \dd''\), then \(\dd^2 = 0\). In our case, we don't have this unless if we put in alternating signs in either of the differentials. We'll follow the convention from Cartan-Eilenberg, which is that we don't write the signs, but remember that they are implied. We imply a \((-1)^p\) where \(p\) is the grading on \(X\). Write
\[A^n = \bigoplus_{p+q = n}A^{p,q}\]
Consider the cohomology of the \emph{total complex} \((A^n, \dd)\).

The strategy is to filter the total cohomology by using subcomplexes \(F^pA\), whose components to the left of the \(p\)-th column are zero. Then
\[(F^pA)^n = \bigoplus_{p' \ge p}A^{p', n - p'}\]
Note \((F^0A)^n = A^n\), and \((F^pA)^n = 0\) for \(p > n\). The inclusions \(F^pA \inj A\) induces a map \(\HH^n(F^pA) \to \HH^n(A)\). We set
\[F^p\HH^n(A) = \im(\HH^n(F^pA) \to \HH^n(A))\]
Thus, we get a filtration
\[\HH^n(A) = F^0\HH^n(A) \supseteq F^1\HH^n(A) \supseteq \cdots \supseteq 0 \tag{\(\dagger\)}\]
The first spectral sequence allows us to calculate
\[\bigoplus_{m=0}^n\frac{F^m\HH^n(A)}{F^{m+1}\HH^n(A)}\]
There is a graded action of \(\HH^n(A)\). Thus, we know \(\HH^n(A)\) as an extension of abelian groups.

Alternatively, we could consider the subcomplex obtained by removing rows from the bottom of our double complex. This will give \(\HH^n(A)\) up to extensions of abelian groups in a (potentially) different way. Fortunately, with our case of the Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence, this second sequence actually only has one non-trivial factor, and so we get that
\[\HH^n(A) = \HH^n(G, M)\]
Thus, the first sequence is giving \(\HH^n(G, M)\), up to extensions of abelian groups. So how do we calculate (\(\dagger\))?

Back to our initial filtration. Let
\[C^{p,q}_r = \{x \in (F^pA)^{p + q} \mid \dd x \in (F^{p+r})^{p+q+1}\}\]
Define for \(r \ge 2\),
\[E^{p,q}_r = \frac{C_r^{p, q} + (F^{p+1}A)^{p+q}}{\dd(C^{p-r+1,q+r-2}_{r-1}) + (F^{p+1}A)^{p+q}}\]
This is called the \emph{\(E_r\)-page}.

\lec

In this case, \(\dd\) induces a map \(\dd_r^{p,q} : E_r^{p, q} \to E_r^{p+r, q-r+1}\), satisfying \(\dd_r^2 = 0\). In practice, we have the \(E_2\)-page with \(d_2\), and then we compute the cohomology
\[\HH(E_2, \dd_2) = E_3\]
in general,
\[\HH(E_r, \dd_r) = E_{r+1}\]

Note that this process must stabilise for a particular coordinate \((p, q)\). For large enough \(r\), the map \(\dd_r\) is going to have image zero when applied to an element with coordinate \((p, q)\). Similarly, for large enough \(r\), the elements whose image under \(\dd_r\) has coordinate \((p, q)\) must be zero. Thus, when we take the cohomology, we are just dealing with zero maps. What we end up is denoted by \(E^{p, q}_\infty\). Note we don't necessarily have an \(r\) where \(E_\infty = E_r\), since the \(r\) for which the terms stabilise depend on \((p, q)\).

\subsection{The \(E_2\)-page}

In the first spectral sequence, we consider \(\HH'(\HH''(A))\), where \(\HH'\) and \(\HH'\) denote cohomology with respect to \(\dd'\) and \(\dd''\) respectively. Since \(\dd' \dd'' + \dd'' \dd' = 0\), the horizontal differential \(\dd'\) induces a differential on \(\HH''(A)\). We can then calculate \(\HH'(\HH''(A))\).

Note that for the second spectral sequence,  we consider \(\HH''(\HH'(A))\) instead. Consider how to compute \(\HH'(\HH''(A))\). Start in the \((p, q)\)-th position. Let \(a^{p, q}\) be a vertical cocycle. So \(\dd'' a^{p, q} = 0\). This defines a class in \(\HH''(A)\), modulo the image under \(\dd''\) of eleents in \((p, q-1)\)-th position. For \(a^{p, q}\) to represent a horizontal cocycle in \(\HH''(A)\) under \(\dd'\), it must be that \(d'a^{p, q}\) (which has coordinates \((p+1, q)\)), is the image under \(\dd''\) of an element \(a^{p+1, q-1}\), with coordinates \((p+1, q-1)\). Thus,
\[\dd(a^{p, q} - a^{p+1, q-1}) = -\dd'a^{p+1, q-1} \in A^{p+2, q-1}\]
Here, we are using \(\dd = \dd' + \dd''\). Hence \(a^{p, q} - a^{p+1, q-1}\) is a cocycle under \(\dd\) modulo everything two-steps to the right of the \((p, q)\)-th position. Similarly, if \(a^{p, q}\) represents coboundary in \(\HH''(A)\) under \(\dd'\), then there are elements \(b^{p-1, q}\) and \(b^{p, q-1}\) such that
\[\begin{cases}
    \dd'' b^{p-1, q} &= 0 \\
    \dd' b^{p-1, q} &= \dd'' b^{p, q-1} + a^{p, q}
\end{cases}\]
Thus,
\[\dd(b^{p-1, q} - b^{p, q-1}) \equiv a^{p, q}\]
modulo eveything everything two to the right of \((p-1, q)\). Thus, the cohomology classes in \(\HH'(\HH''(A))\) gives elements of the \(E_2\)-page, and vice versa. The upshot is that the \(E_2\)-page is \(\HH'(\HH''(A))\), together with \(\dd_2\) which is induced by \(\dd\).

Suppose \(a \in A^n\) is a cocycle with respect to \(\dd\), starting in \(A^{p, q}\), where \(p + q = n\). That is, \(a \in (F^pA)^n \setminus (F^{p+1}A)^n\). Then \(\dd a = 0\), and so we get an element in \(E^{p, q}_r\) for all \(r\). Since \(\dd_r\) is induced by \(\dd\), \(\dd_r a = 0\). Thus, \(a\) determines an element of \(E_\infty^{p, q}\), and thus we have a map
\[F^p\HH^{p+q}(A) \to E^{p,q}_\infty\]
This map is surjective, and so
\[E_\infty^{p, q} = \bigoplus_{p, n}\frac{F^p\HH^n(A)}{F^{p+1}\HH^n(A)}\]
This is a graded version of \(\HH^*(A)\). In the particular case of the Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre sequences, \(A = \Hom_Q(Y^\bullet, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M))\). Here,
\begin{align*}
    \dd' &= \Hom_Q(d_Y, \id) \\
    \dd'' &= \Hom_Q(\id, \dd_X^*)
\end{align*}
Recall we have suppressed the alternating sign in \(\dd''\). Now
\[\HH''(A) = \Hom_Q(Y, \HH^*(\Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)))\]
Since terms of \(Y^\bullet\) are \(\Z Q\)-projective, and so \(\Hom_Q(Y, \cdot)\) preserves exactness. Thus,
\[E_2 = \HH'\HH''(A) = \HH^*(\Hom_Q(Y^\bullet, \HH^*(H, M))) = \HH^*(Q, \HH^*(H, M))\]
Recall \(\HH^*(H, M)\) is a \(\Z Q\)-module so this makes sense. The \(\dd_2\) is induced from \(\dd\).

For the second spectral sequence, we have that the \(E_2\)-page is given by \(\HH''(\HH'(A))\), and we have
\[\HH'(\Hom_Q(Y^\bullet, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)))= \HH^*(Q, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M))\]

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:cohoology-vanishing}
    \(\HH^p(Q, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)) = 0\) for \(p > 0\).
\end{lemma}

We'll prove this later. Thus, \(\HH'(A)\) is concentrated on the line \(p = 0\). On this line,
\[\HH^0(Q, \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)) = \Hom_H(X^\bullet, M)^Q = \Hom_G(X^\bullet, M)\]
Hence
\[\HH''(\HH'(A)) = \HH^*(\Hom_G(X^\bullet, M)) = \HH^*(G, M)\]

Thus, for the second spectral sequence, the \(E_2\)-page is concentrated on the line \(p = 0\). It follows \(E_r = E_\infty\) for \(r \ge 2\). So \(E_\infty\) is also concentrated on the line \(p = 0\). Hence the filtration of \(\HH^n(A)\) has only one non-trivial factor. Thus, we get
\[\HH^n(A) = \HH^n(G, M)\]
for each \(n\).

\lec

\begin{proof}
    [Proof of \cref{lem:cohoology-vanishing}]
    Since each \(X^q\) is \(\ZG\)-projective, it is a direct summand of a free module. It suffices to prove the lemma for \(\ZG\). Let \(\widetilde M\) be \(M\) as an additive group, but with trivial \(\ZG\)-module structure. We claim that there is an isomorphism of \(\ZG\)-modules
    \[\Hom_H(\ZG, M) \cong \Hom_H(\ZG, \widetilde M)\]
    where \(G\) acts on the left hand side by \((gf)(x) = g(f(g^{-1}x))\), and on the right hand side as in the coinduced module on examples sheet 1. That is, \((gf)(x) = f(xg)\).

    To see this, for \(f \in \Hom_H(\ZG, M)\), define \(f' \in \Hom_H(\ZG, \widetilde M)\) by
    \[f'(x) = xf(x^{-1})\]
    for \(x \in G\). We leave the check that \(f'\) is well defined, \((f')' = f\) and this gives a \(\ZG\)-module isomorphism.

    This claim allows us to use Shapiro's lemma, and noting that we have an isomorphism
    \[\Hom_H(\ZG, \widetilde M) = \Hom(\Z Q, \widetilde M)\]
    since \(H\) acts trivially. Thus,
    \[\HH^p(Q, \Hom_H(\ZG, M)) \cong \HH^p(Q, \Hom(\Z Q, \widetilde M)) \cong \HH^p(1, \widetilde M) = 0\]
    for \(p > 0\), where Shapiro's lemma says that
    \[\HH^p(H, N) = \HH^p(G, \coind_G^H(M))\]
\end{proof}

Recall the \(\Z Q\)-module structure on \(\HH^n(H, M)\) is as follows:

Let \(G\) act on cochains \(C^n(H, M)\) by
\[(g\cdot\phi)(h_1, \dots, h_n) = g\phi(g^{-1}hg, \dots, g^{-1}h_ng)\]
This descends to an action of \(G\) on \(\HH^n(H, M)\). Under this, \(H\) acts trivially, and so we have a \(Q\) action. Here, we're only interested in \(\HH^1(H, M)\), and so if \(\phi : H \to M\) is a derivation, representing the cohomology class \([\phi]\), then
\[[\phi] \in \HH^1(H, M)^{Q} \iff [\phi] = [g\phi]\]
for all \(g \in G\). If \(M\) is a trivial \(\ZG\)-module, then \(\HH^1(H, M) = \Hom(H_\ab, M)\), and the fixed points are the \(\Z Q\)-maps.

For the maps in \cref{cor:five-term-exact-sequence-group-cohomology}, we have restriction maps \(\HH^n(G, M) \to \HH^n(H, M)^Q\), where we define the maps on cochains by
\[f \in C^n(G, M) \mapsto \res f = f\vert_{H^n} \in C^n(H, M)\]
which then induces a map on cohomology.

Next, we have inflation maps \(\HH^n(Q, M^H) \to \HH^n(G, M)\), defined on cochain as follows:
\[f \in C^n(Q, M^H) \mapsto \inf f \in C^n(G, M)\]
given by

    T_g(f)(g_1, g_2) &= f(\rho(g_1)\rho(g_2)) - f(\rho(g_1g_2))
\end{align*}
Note that changing \(g_1, g_2\) by multiplication by elements of \(H\) does not change tthe value. So we can define a cochain \(T_g(f) : Q^2 \to M\). The image lies in \(M^H\) and it is a cocycle. Thus, we have a map
\[[f] \in \HH^1(H, M)^Q \mapsto [T_g(f)] \in \HH^2(Q, M^H)\]
